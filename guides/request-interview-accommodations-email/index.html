Interview accommodation request emails: six templates by scenario

2026 · ~7 min read · OfferPilot AI

Send the request as a reply on the scheduling thread, in the same message where you accept a time slot, and write three sentences: the functional limitation in terms of how you work, the one specific change you want, and what stays the same. Do not name a diagnosis, and do not leave it until the night before, because the most common accommodations are settings applied when your assessment invite is generated or bookings made through an outside agency. Six emails you can paste and edit are below, with the lead time each one needs and a follow-up sequence for when nobody replies.

This is wording help, not legal advice. The ADA calls these reasonable accommodations, the Equality Act 2010 calls them reasonable adjustments, and both weigh the barrier and the change rather than the name of a condition. What applies to you depends on where you are, the employer and the role.

The sentence formula: functional limitation, not diagnosis

A recruiting coordinator cannot act on a diagnosis. They can act on a change to a calendar entry, a platform setting or a meeting format. So convert the label into the mechanic it affects, then say what does not change, which settles the only real objection on the other side, fairness to other candidates.

  • Instead of "I have ADHD", write "I take in questions more accurately when I can read them as well as hear them".
  • Instead of "I am autistic", write "I answer direct questions well and unpack indirect ones slowly, so knowing the competency areas in advance lets me answer the question you are actually asking".
  • Instead of "I have a stutter", write "My speech is fluent but slower under time pressure, so a hard timer measures my speech rate rather than my reasoning".
  • Instead of "I am hard of hearing", write "I rely on lip reading and captions, so I need cameras on and a platform where I have tested the caption quality".
  • Instead of "I have an anxiety disorder", write "I perform closer to my actual level when a long session has a scheduled break in it".

The closing sentence in every template below does the same job: the content, the scope and the standard I am assessed against are unchanged. That single line is the difference between a coordinator ticking a box and a coordinator forwarding your email to someone senior for a decision.

Timing: at scheduling, not the night before

Reply on the existing thread

Do not open a new email with a new subject line. The scheduling thread is the one the coordinator is actually watching, and a fresh subject gets triaged as a general enquiry. Keeping it in one thread also leaves a written trail with dates on it.

Ask before the invite is generated

Extended time on an assessment platform is usually a setting attached to your invite rather than something toggled mid-test. If the invite has already landed, ask for it to be voided and reissued instead of starting the test and asking afterwards.

Ask who owns it in the same breath

One line: "Is there a dedicated accommodations contact for this process, or are you the right person?" Larger employers often have one, and routing yourself to them saves the two days the forward would have cost.

Get it into the calendar invite

An adjustment agreed in email and never written into the invite frequently does not reach the panel, because interviewers read the invite and nothing else. Ask explicitly: "Could this be noted in the calendar invite so the panel sees it?"

The six emails

Each one is short on purpose. Two asks at most, mechanics only, no apology and no medical detail. Replace the bracketed parts and delete anything that does not apply to you.

1. Extra time on a timed assessment

Subject: Re: [existing thread], adjustment before the assessment invite

Hi [Name], happy to take the assessment. Before the invite is generated, I would like to request one adjustment. I read and work through written problems more slowly than the average candidate, so could the assessment be issued with extended time, 1.5x if that is a standard option on your platform? Same problems, same scope, same standard. If there is a dedicated accommodations contact who applies these settings, please point me to them or copy them in.

What usually happens: granted when it arrives first, awkward when it arrives second. Naming a multiplier is what makes it easy, because the person reading it has a dropdown in front of them and you have picked an option from it.

2. Questions or topics in advance

Subject: Re: [existing thread], format question for [date]

Hi [Name], two small things that would help me prepare properly. Could you share the competency areas the interview will cover and the structure of the session, for example how many questions and whether any part is live coding? And during the call, could each question be pasted into the chat as it is asked? I take in questions more accurately when I can read them as well as hear them. Neither changes the questions themselves or the time I spend answering.

What usually happens: both are commonly granted. The exact question list is not, because interviewers hear that as an unfair advantage, so ask for these two narrower versions by name rather than for the thing that gets refused.

3. Written, or video with chat, instead of a phone screen

Subject: Re: [existing thread], format for the screening call

Hi [Name], could we run the screen as a video call with chat enabled rather than a phone call? Audio only strips the visual cues I rely on to follow a conversation, and the chat gives us a fallback for names, numbers and spellings. If video is not possible, I am happy to answer the same screening questions in writing on the same deadline. Content and length unchanged either way.

What usually happens: the video swap is close to automatic, since most recruiters would rather be on video anyway. Written answers are a bigger ask, so offer them as the fallback rather than as the opening position.

4. Scheduled breaks in a long loop

Subject: Re: [existing thread], break in the [date] loop

Hi [Name], the loop runs [three hours] back to back. I perform closer to my actual level with a short scheduled break, so could we build in five minutes between the second and third sessions, and could that be noted in the calendar invite so each interviewer sees it? It does not shorten any session, I am happy for the loop to end five minutes later.

What usually happens: the easiest yes on this page and almost never refused outright. The failure mode is not refusal, it is a granted break that nobody passes on to the panel, which is why the invite matters more than the reply.

5. Captions or an interpreter

Subject: Adjustment request, [Role] interview [date], interpreter booking

Hi [Name], I use [a BSL or ASL interpreter / live captions] for interviews. Could you confirm who arranges and covers the booking on your side? The session is [60] minutes on [platform]. I rely on lip reading alongside the interpreter, so I would also ask that interviewers keep cameras on and that one person speaks at a time. If it is easier, I can share the details of an agency I have worked with before.

What usually happens: this is the ask with the longest lead time, because it involves a third party and often a purchase order. State the session length up front, since interpreters commonly work in pairs on longer assignments and that changes the booking. Arrangement and cost sitting with the employer is the norm, so ask the question rather than offering to pay.

6. A take-home instead of a live coding round

Subject: Re: [existing thread], format of the technical round

Hi [Name], could I propose a swap for the live coding round? Being watched while typing changes how I work, but I want you to get the same signal, so I would suggest the same problem as a timeboxed take-home, [90] minutes, followed by a walkthrough call where the interviewer can question my code line by line and ask me to extend it live. Same problem, same rubric, and you still see me reason about it in real time.

What usually happens: more mixed than the others, and it depends on whether the team already runs a take-home track. It moves far more often when framed as a swap than as a removal, because the recruiter needs something concrete to take to the hiring manager, and "she will still walk us through it live" is that something.

When nobody replies

Silence is the normal outcome, not refusal, and it is usually a coordinator holding forty threads rather than a decision about you. Escalate in three short steps, all on the same thread.

  1. Two working days later. "Hi [Name], just bumping this one. The only thing I need is [the extended time setting], and it has to be applied before the invite goes out, so could you confirm whether that sits with you or with an accommodations contact? Happy to email them directly."
  2. Twenty four hours out, if it is still silent. "Hi [Name], as I have not heard back I will assume [the five minute break after the second session] is fine and will mention it to the panel at the start. Do let me know if that is a problem."
  3. On the day, to the interviewer. One sentence in the first minute: "I asked the recruiting team for a short break at the midpoint, is that noted on your side?" Say it to the person in front of you rather than chasing the thread, and write down what they answer.

Two things are worth doing regardless. Keep the whole request in one thread so the dates are visible if you ever need them, and re-confirm the day before, because an adjustment that was granted and never reached the panel is the single most common way this goes wrong.

Where this advice stops

Some of these requests will not be granted, and a few will be answered in a way that tells you something useful about the employer. That is better to learn now than after an offer. A refusal in writing is also more use to you than a vague verbal one, so if you are told no, reply asking for the decision to be confirmed by email.
