Chrome extension vs desktop interview copilot: what each one shows

2026 · ~9 min read · OfferPilot AI

A copilot can live in exactly three kinds of place, and the place decides what an interviewer receives. A second browser tab stays out of a tab share but appears in the tab strip the moment you share the browser window. An extension panel splits in two: a browser side panel escapes a tab share, while a panel injected into the page is inside the shared tab by definition. A native window excluded from capture at the operating system level is absent from all three modes, because the compositor removes it before the frame ever reaches Zoom.

The three places a copilot can live

People compare "extension versus app" as though it were a packaging decision. It is not. It is a decision about which rectangle your helper is drawn inside, and every screen sharing mode captures a different rectangle.

  • A browser tab. A web app you open in a second tab and read by switching to it, or by tiling two Chrome windows side by side. No install, no permissions, nothing sitting on the machine, and no way to look at it without leaving the tab you are working in.
  • An extension surface. Three different things wear this one label. A toolbar popup, which closes the instant it loses focus and is therefore useless while you type. A side panel, drawn by Chrome along the edge of the window, beside the page. And an in-page panel injected by a content script, which is ordinary HTML added to the interviewer's page, floating over the editor.
  • A native window. A normal desktop window, which can additionally be marked so that Windows strips it out of any capture before the capturing application receives a single frame. It stays lit on your own monitor and is simply not in the encoded video.

That last mechanism is the only one on this page that is not a matter of geometry. The other surfaces are hidden or visible depending on where the capture rectangle happens to fall. An excluded window is removed by the operating system regardless of where it sits, what it overlaps, or which mode you picked.

Every cell describes what the other participants receive, not what your own monitor shows. Read the tab share column first, because it is the mode most interview platforms ask for and the one people most often assume is safe.

The injected panel trap

Row four is the one worth stopping on. Advice about screen sharing converges on "share a single tab, it leaks the least", and for browser chrome that is true: the address bar, the bookmarks bar, your other tabs and the downloads bubble all stay out of the frame. But a content script writes into the same document the page renders. Whatever it draws is page content, and page content is exactly what a tab share is designed to send.

There is a second consequence that has nothing to do with video. Because an injected panel lives in the page's own DOM tree, the page's own JavaScript can observe it. Any script on that origin can walk the document, or watch it with a mutation observer, and see nodes it did not create. Whether a particular assessment platform actually does that is a question about that platform, and I am not going to assert a specific behaviour for one. The structural point stands without it: a side panel and a native window are outside the document, and an injected panel is inside it.

So the ranking flips depending on the mode. Under a tab share, an injected panel is the worst of the six surfaces and a side panel is fine. Under a window share, the side panel is fully visible and a second tab is barely better. Under a full screen share, everything except an OS excluded window is on camera.

Where the interviewer's voice actually comes from

Visibility is only half the comparison. A copilot that cannot hear the question cannot answer it, and this is where the two architectures diverge hardest.

Prove all of this on your own machine in six minutes

Do not take a vendor's word for any row above, mine included. Every claim on this page is testable before it matters.

Start a solo meeting and join it from your phone

Google Meet or Zoom, with the phone muted and its speaker off so nothing echoes. The phone is standing in for your interviewer and it is the only honest evidence in the room.

Open the copilot surface you actually plan to use

Not a screenshot from a product page. The real panel, in the browser profile you will interview from, on the editor page you will be given.

Share one tab, then look at the phone

An injected panel appears here. A side panel, a popup and a second tab do not. This single test settles most of the argument.

Stop, share the Chrome window, look again

Now the side panel, the extension icons and every tab title are in frame. If you only ever test tab share, the day an interviewer asks for a window will be a surprise.

Share your entire screen, look again

Everything except an OS excluded window is now transmitted, notification toasts and taskbar badges included.

Take a screenshot with Win+Shift+S and open the saved file

Screenshot and recording paths are not the same code as a live share. Judge it on the image on disk, not the preview thumbnail.

For a browser based coding round, the arrangement that survives all three modes is a shared editor tab plus a native window beside it. That is how Code Copilot is meant to be used: the problem stays in the tab you are sharing, and the working notes stay in a window that is not in the frame.

What the extension model genuinely does better

Three real advantages, none of them about screen sharing.

  • It installs where an executable cannot. A locked down corporate laptop that refuses installers will often still allow an extension from the store. If you cannot install software on the machine you interview from, the comparison is already over.
  • It runs on hardware a Windows app never will. ChromeOS, macOS, a Linux desktop. A Chromebook has no native option at all, so the choice there is an extension or nothing.
  • It asks nothing of the operating system. No loopback audio device, no window level flags, no background process. For some people that is the deciding factor and it is a reasonable one.

What it cannot do is opt out of a capture, because that decision is made by the window manager about a window, and an extension does not have one to mark. Any comparison that skips past this is quietly comparing convenience with convenience.

Where OfferPilot is the wrong tool

Plainly, so nobody discovers it at the worst moment. There is no browser extension. It is Windows 10 and 11 only, with no macOS, Linux, ChromeOS or mobile build. On a Chromebook, on a Mac, or on a machine where IT blocks installers, an extension based product is the better fit, and the table above is the honest reason why.

Capture exclusion also has a hard boundary that gets blurred in marketing everywhere. It covers screen sharing, screen recording and screenshots. It does nothing about a webcam, a lockdown browser that owns the whole machine, a proctor watching you in person, eye tracking, or a phone propped up as a second camera. Eyes travelling repeatedly to one side read as exactly that on video, whatever is or is not inside the share. And in a proctored assessment running inside a lockdown browser, none of this applies at all. That is not the situation these tools are for.

Choosing, in two sentences

If the interview audio comes out of a desktop client, and any of the three share modes could be asked for, a native window that the operating system keeps out of the frame is the only surface that behaves identically in all three columns. If you cannot install software, or you are not on Windows, take a side panel over an injected panel, keep the copilot out of the tab you are sharing, and treat a window share request as the moment to close it.
