Huru AI alternative: rehearsal reps before the call, a copilot inside it

2026 · ~6 min read · OfferPilot AI

Most people searching for a Huru AI alternative are looking in the wrong aisle. Huru sits in the rehearsal category: timed mock questions, a recorded answer, a score on how you delivered it. That work happens in the week before. A live copilot does something structurally different, it sits in the real call and drafts an answer while a stranger is waiting. Neither replaces the other, and the honest split up front is this: OfferPilot AI has no version of delivery scoring at all. No filler-word count, no pacing, nothing.

The two stages, and which tool owns each

Preparation has a shape: question harvesting, rep volume, delivery polish, the call itself, then review. A rehearsal app owns the first three and the last one. A live copilot owns exactly one stage, the call, and is useless in the others. The mistake is buying a tool for stage four and expecting it to do stage two.

Ten to five days out: rep volume

This belongs entirely to a mock interview app. Huru-style tools generate questions from a job posting and put you under a timer, and the point is repetition. Saying your promotion story eight times out loud is what makes it arrive in four seconds on the day. A copilot cannot do this, because it does not exist until the interview starts.

Four to two days out: delivery polish

Also rehearsal territory, and specifically the part OfferPilot does not touch. Filler words per minute, whether you speed up when nervous, whether you trail off at the end of an answer. You need a recording of yourself and a metric to compare takes against. A live copilot gives you neither.

The night before: content gaps

The two overlap here for the first time. A practice app shows which answers still come out shapeless, and a company-specific question bank is worth building now, so the live layer has real material rather than a job title.

During the call: the unrehearsed question

Every practice app is closed by now. This is the only stage a live copilot exists for, and what it is good at is the question that was not on your list, plus the follow-up two levels deeper than the one you prepared.

Afterwards: review

Rehearsal apps win again, because they kept the tape. The copilot keeps no recording of you, which is good for privacy and bad for learning. If you want to know why the third answer landed badly, it has nothing to show you.

A worked fortnight, if you use both

Say a final round is fourteen days out, a behavioural panel then a technical screen. A sensible split looks like this rather than picking a winner.

  • Days 14 to 9. Rehearsal app only. Twenty timed reps across six stories. Ignore the delivery scores for now, since scoring a half-written answer tells you nothing.
  • Days 8 to 5. Now read the delivery metrics. If the filler-word count spikes on only two stories, those two stories are underwritten rather than badly delivered. Rewrite them, then re-rep.
  • Days 4 to 2. Build the company question bank from the job posting and your resume with the Q&A generator, so the live layer has something specific to draw on.
  • Day 1. One dry run of the setup. Test call with a friend, share your screen, confirm the overlay is absent from what they see, and check audio still routes with your headphones in.
  • Day 0. Practice app closed. The copilot covers the questions your twenty reps did not.

When a live copilot is the wrong purchase entirely

Three cases, common enough to state plainly rather than bury near the footer.

If you know what to say and it comes out badly, buy the rehearsal app and skip this one. Reading a good answer off a screen does not fix a shaky voice, a habit of answering a slightly different question from the one asked, or an answer that runs four minutes when it should run ninety seconds. Delivery is a motor skill, and the only fix is reps with feedback, which is precisely the thing that does not exist here.

If you interview on a Mac or from your phone, the comparison ends at the platform row. There is no Mac build and no web version standing in for one, because the screen-share exclusion depends on Windows compositing behaviour. Mobile-first practice apps genuinely win here: they follow you around, and a copilot tied to the meeting machine cannot.

If your interview is a one-way recorded video, a rehearsal tool adds a lot and a copilot adds almost nothing, since the whole exercise is delivery to a camera with no interviewer speech to hear.

What the hidden overlay actually covers

Capture exclusion means the overlay is composited out of screen sharing and screen recording at the operating system level. Zoom, Teams, Meet, OBS and a plain screenshot show your desktop without it, while it stays visible on your own monitor. That is the entire claim. It does not defeat a webcam watching your eyes, a lockdown browser, an in-person proctor, eye tracking, or a phone filming you from the side of the desk. Where a process forbids assistance, no software changes that. Which is one more reason the rehearsal half matters: preparation works everywhere, under every rule, on every platform.

Deciding in two questions

Ask what actually goes wrong for you. If it is "I freeze and then ramble", that is a rehearsal problem and a mock interview app is the right purchase, whatever any vendor page says. If it is "I get asked something sideways in minute thirty", no volume of reps closes that, because the question was never on your list. Most people in a serious search have both.
