Captions for a remote interview: CART, auto-captions, an interpreter, or on-device transcription

2026 · ~7 min read · OfferPilot AI

Ask for CART in writing, to the recruiting coordinator, at the moment the interview is being scheduled. Of the four options below it is the only one that is reliably accurate on proper nouns and technical vocabulary, and the only one you can prime with a glossary beforehand. Platform auto-captions are free and need nobody's permission, but they break on exactly the words a technical round turns on. An interpreter is the right ask if a signed language is your first language, and personal on-device transcription is a self-help layer you run for yourself, never a substitute for the accommodation you were entitled to request.

This is practical wording, not legal advice. In the United States the ADA treats CART and interpreting as reasonable accommodations that the employer arranges and pays for, and it is not lawful to push the cost onto the candidate. In the UK these are reasonable adjustments under the Equality Act 2010, and the Access to Work scheme has historically funded communication support at interview even before you are anyone's employee, so check the current terms rather than assuming. Either way, do not book a captioner on your own card and hope to claim it back.

The four options, scored on what actually decides a round

Latency and cost are the columns people ask about. Accuracy on names and jargon is the column that decides whether you answer the question that was asked, and consent is the column that decides whether you have any recourse when it fails.

The glossary is the reason CART is accurate, so send one

This is the step most candidates skip, and it is the difference between a transcript you can answer from and one you spend the round decoding. Captioners write from a personal dictionary built up over years, and unfamiliar proper nouns are the known weak point. Every agency will accept a prep pack. Most want it at least a day ahead, so it is loaded before the session rather than during it.

  • Names and roles from the calendar invite. Attendee names as they are spelled, plus job titles. A mangled surname in the transcript is a small thing until you have to use it in a thank-you email.
  • The company's own vocabulary. Internal product names, team names, anything from the careers page that would not appear in a general dictionary.
  • The stack, copied verbatim from the job advert. Kubernetes, Kafka, gRPC, Terraform, whatever is listed. Paste it rather than retyping from memory, so the spelling is theirs.
  • The acronyms in your own resume. You will say them out loud, and the interviewer will repeat them back with a follow-up question attached.
  • Ask for verbatim CART, not meaning-for-meaning. Condensed services such as TypeWell and C-Print are excellent in a lecture, where the point is the gist. In a system design round the clause that gets condensed away is the constraint the answer is scored on. Put the word verbatim in the request.
  • Ask whether you get the transcript afterwards. Often you can, and it makes a specific follow-up email trivial to write. Ask rather than assume, because a saved transcript raises a consent question the coordinator has to settle first.

The booking sequence, and where it breaks

At scheduling, name the service and the format

Not "I have hearing loss", which invites a well-meaning guess. "Verbatim CART, delivered as a streaming text link I open in a second window" is a request a coordinator can forward to a vendor without a single follow-up email.

Get the delivery method confirmed in writing

In-meeting captioning depends on the employer's account settings and on whether an admin has enabled third-party captions at all. A separate streaming text window depends on nothing but a browser. If they offer both, take the browser link as primary and treat the in-meeting version as the spare.

Send the glossary two days out

To the coordinator, with an explicit ask to forward it to the captioning agency. Coordinators file attachments and forget them, so name the deadline: "could this reach the captioner by Thursday so it can be loaded in advance".

Join fifteen minutes early for a caption check

Test the link, the font size and the window position while it is still housekeeping. Fifteen minutes is enough to escalate to a phone call if the link is dead. Two minutes is not.

Re-confirm per round, and ask about overrun

The classic failure on a four round loop is coverage for round one only. Ask two questions in one message: is the captioner booked for every session including the gaps, and what happens if the loop runs thirty minutes long.

The request email

Subject: Interview accommodation request, [Role], [date]

Hi [Name],

Thanks for setting this up. I am hard of hearing and I would like to request CART captioning for the interview. Is there an accommodations contact I should copy in, or are you the right person to arrange it?

What I need is verbatim CART, ideally delivered as a streaming text link I can open in a second browser window, since that works regardless of the meeting platform. If the captioner can be booked for all four sessions rather than the first one, that covers the whole loop in one go.

I will send a short glossary of names and technical terms two days beforehand so it can be loaded in advance. Could you confirm the vendor once it is booked? I will join fifteen minutes early to test the link.

Best regards,
[Your name]

Three things that email does deliberately. It names a service rather than a difficulty, so nobody has to research what to buy. It offers the glossary, which reads as helpful and is the single biggest lever on accuracy. And it raises the whole loop up front, before an agency quote has been signed off for one hour only.

Before you rely on platform captions

Auto-captions are worth switching on in every call, including the ones that already have CART, because they cost nothing and cover the seconds where a human misses. Two specifics decide whether they can be your primary. First, the feature can simply be missing: on a locked-down corporate account an administrator can disable captions, so open a test meeting on the exact platform named in the invite and confirm the control is there. Second, they degrade badly on a panel. Two people finishing each other's sentences is the situation every automatic system handles worst, and it is also the situation you most need help with. Find the caption control before the call starts, because hunting through a menu during a live question costs you the question.

Where a personal transcription tool fits, and where it does not

Say this part plainly: a personal transcription tool is a self-help layer, not an accommodation. It is never a substitute for a formally requested CART service, and it should not become your reason for skipping the email above. The request is the thing that changes the process, creates a record, and gives you something to point at when a round is run without access. Software on your own laptop does none of that, and if you quietly use it instead of asking, the next employer's process has exactly the same gap in it.

The honest limits of the on-device option, ours included:

  • It produces an English transcript. If BSL or ASL is your first language, text in a second language is not equivalent access, and no amount of speed changes that. Ask for the interpreter.
  • Speech models invent things. Whisper can emit fluent, confident text during silence or noise that nobody said. If a line surprises you, treat it as unverified and ask, rather than answering the sentence you read.
  • Crosstalk defeats it, exactly as it defeats platform captions. Separating simultaneous speakers is the hard part of the problem and none of the automatic options solve it.
  • The audio has to come through the computer. Loopback capture reads system playback, so headphones and AirPods are fine, but a phone dial-in is out. If a round moves to a phone line the transcript stops.
  • Windows 10 and 11 only. No macOS, no Linux, no mobile, no browser extension, so an in-person interview or a MacBook is outside what this can do at all.
  • Capture exclusion is a privacy property, not an anti-detection one. The overlay is excluded from screen capture at the operating system level, so it does not appear in Zoom, Teams, Meet, OBS or a screenshot. That covers screen sharing and recording only. It does nothing about a webcam, a lockdown browser, eye tracking, a second-device camera, or someone watching you in the room.

If the answer is no, or is silence

Get it in writing, then narrow the ask. A refusal to fund CART is rarely a refusal to do anything at all, and the cheaper adjustments need no vendor: one interviewer speaking at a time rather than a panel, each question also pasted into the chat as it is asked, a written technical prompt instead of a spoken one, the agenda and attendee list in advance, next steps confirmed by email. Ask them to enable the platform's own captions if an admin policy is blocking them, which is a settings change rather than a purchase. And say the sentence that removes the fairness objection before it gets raised: none of this changes the content of the interview or the time you spend answering.
