AI interview copilot for new grad software engineers: which rounds it actually helps

2026 · ~6 min read · OfferPilot AI

A new grad SDE loop has five distinct stages, and a live copilot is genuinely useful in three of them: the recruiter screen, the technical phone screen and the behavioural round. It is the wrong tool for a webcam-proctored online assessment, because capture exclusion hides an overlay from screen sharing, not from a camera. On the free tier, five minutes a day of listening time is best spent as exactly one behavioural question, drilled daily, rather than sprayed across a whole mock loop.

The five stages, and what a copilot can and cannot do in each

The new grad pipeline at large employers is more standardised than the experienced-hire one, which makes it easy to map, split honestly between what a live assistant changes and what it does not.

Stage What it usually is What OfferPilot does here What it does not do
Recruiter screen 20 to 30 minutes, no code. Graduation date, work authorisation, location, "walk me through your resume", sometimes a compensation expectation. Transcribes the recruiter on device and drafts a resume-grounded answer in about a second, so you do not blank on your own capstone or which term your internship ran. It does not know your visa status or the number you want. Decide those before you dial.
Online assessment 60 to 120 minutes of auto-graded algorithm problems on a timer. Frequently webcam-recorded, sometimes inside a lockdown browser, sometimes both. Nothing during the assessment. Its role is beforehand: run practice sets through the screenshot capture and read the Big-O walkthrough until the pattern is yours. Capture exclusion hides the overlay from screen sharing and recording only. It does not defeat a webcam, a lockdown browser, eye tracking or a phone propped behind you. Keep it closed.
Technical phone screen 45 minutes in a shared editor such as CoderPad or HackerRank, one medium problem or two easy ones, with a human watching you type. Reads a problem that spans several scrolls, then returns intuition, a dry run and complexity. Gemini is the recommended model here. It does not narrate for you. This round scores reasoning out loud, and silent typing followed by correct code scores worse than a messy solution you explained.
Virtual onsite Three to five back-to-back rounds in one day: two or three coding, often a project deep dive, sometimes a light design or debugging round. Metering is listening time only, so a four-hour day spends clock only while an interviewer is actually speaking, a fraction of the wall clock. The deep dive is about code you wrote. No draft recalls why you picked one database over another in your final-year project.
Behavioural round 30 to 45 minutes of STAR questions against a values rubric, such as Amazon Leadership Principles. The round new grads underprepare for. The strongest fit of the five. A company-specific question bank plus your resume means the draft names your actual project, not a template. It cannot invent experience. The fix for a thin story bank is three or four flexible stories rehearsed properly, not a fresh one read live.

Why the proctored OA is the one stage to keep it closed

This is the question new grads ask most and the one vendors answer least clearly, so no hedging. The overlay is excluded from screen capture at the operating system level: the compositor removes it from the frame before Zoom, Teams, Meet, OBS or a screenshot tool receives one, so it is absent from a share and from a recording while staying visible on your own monitor.

That is a narrow guarantee about pixels in a capture stream, and it is the whole of it. It says nothing about a webcam, which sees your face and your eye movement rather than your display. Nothing about a lockdown browser, which is built to take control of the machine and watch what runs on it. Nothing about a second-device camera pointed at your desk, a live proctor, or a room scan.

So a webcam-proctored assessment is not a place for a live assistant. Prepare for it, then sit it clean. New grad assessments draw from a narrow band of patterns anyway: two pointers, sliding window, hash-map counting, BFS on a grid, a straightforward DP. Drilling those beforehand beats anything you could do during the ninety minutes.

Five free minutes a day, spent as one behavioural drill

The free tier gives five minutes a day with no card, and what makes it usable is how the clock works: it advances only while the copilot is receiving interviewer audio, not for the whole session. A question and its follow-ups are a small share of any exchange, and the two minutes you spend answering cost nothing. Five minutes therefore buys roughly one full behavioural question a day.

Build the bank once

Use the company Q&A generator with your resume, the target company and the role title exactly as the posting writes it, usually "Software Engineer, New Grad" or "SDE I". Once, not daily.

Run one question live

Have the question spoken into your system audio, on a mock call with a friend or from your own recording. Loopback capture means it still works through headphones, and only that speech spends clock.

Answer before you read

Speak your version out loud first, timed, then look at the draft. The other way round, the drill teaches you to read from a screen, which is the habit that fails in a real round.

Keep only the delta

Note the one thing the draft had that you missed, usually a quantified result or a clean statement of the conflict, fold it into the story permanently, then stop the session.

Twenty weekdays is twenty questions, enough to cover the recurring themes: a team disagreement, a missed deadline, a bug you owned, something you taught yourself. Cross-check against the company question banks so you drill what the company actually asks.

Shortcuts worth knowing for a coding round

New grad phone screens like to paste a long problem statement with three worked examples and a wall of constraints, which will not fit on one screen. Code Copilot handles that with a multi-shot capture: Ctrl+Alt+S starts it, you drag a box, you get about three seconds to scroll, you drag again, and you repeat until the whole problem is captured. Enter sends every shot together, Esc cancels. OCR runs server-side, so text-only models still read the statement.

Two other habits pay off live. Hold P to make the overlay see-through when you need to read the editor underneath, and use Ctrl+Alt+Q to cycle answer mode, since a rapid-fire behavioural round wants Fast while a gnarly algorithm question wants Smart. The new grad follow-up is almost always "what is the runtime, and can you do better", so read the Big-O line before you speak.

Where this is the wrong tool for a new grad

Four cases, stated plainly.

  • You interview from a MacBook. Windows 10 and 11 only. No macOS build, no Linux, no mobile app, no browser extension. A large share of computer science students are on a Mac, and nothing about the product changes that.
  • Proctored or in-person rounds. Covered above for the assessment, and the same applies to on-campus final rounds, career fair whiteboards and anything on paper. There is no screen to overlay.
  • Fundamentals you have not built. New grad loops are calibrated on how you reason under a follow-up, not on final code. "Why a heap here", "what happens at ten million elements", "what breaks if the input is already sorted". Interviewers probe new grads harder precisely because there is less work history to judge, and a read answer collapses on the second probe.
  • Take-homes with a disclosure policy. More employers now ask candidates to declare AI use on take-home projects. Read the brief and follow it.

The useful frame: preparation and live support for rounds where notes are normal, plus an accessibility aid if you interview in a second language or freeze under pressure. Not a way around an assessment designed to be sat unaided. Free covers the daily drill; the daily limits only matter once a full onsite is in the diary.
