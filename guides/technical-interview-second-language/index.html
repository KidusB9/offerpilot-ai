Technical interviews in English as a second language: 40 phrases that buy you room

2026 · ~7 min read · OfferPilot AI

The problem in a second-language technical interview is rarely your accent. It is the three seconds where you are still translating while the interviewer has moved to the next constraint. Rehearsed phrases fix that faster than better English does: eight to clarify, eight to buy time, eight to signpost, eight to self-correct, eight for when the word will not come.

Say one sentence at the start, then never again: "English is my second language, so I may need a second to find the exact word. Tell me if anything is unclear." That sets an expectation without asking for allowance. Do not repeat it, and do not apologise mid-answer.

The 40 phrases, sorted by the moment you need them

Rehearse them aloud. A phrase you have only read will not arrive under pressure, and six do most of the work.

Clarify, before you commit to an answer

  1. "Let me repeat the problem back, and you correct me if I have it wrong."
  2. "Could you say the last part again? I want the constraint exactly."
  3. "When you say scale, do you mean requests per second or total data stored?"
  4. "Is that a follow-up, or a new question?"
  5. "Was that consistent or persistent? Those two sound close to me."
  6. "Could you spell that name? I want to write it down correctly."
  7. "Do you want the approach first, or the code first?"
  8. "So A and B are fixed, and the rest is mine to choose. Is that right?"

Buy time without dead air

  1. "Give me ten seconds to think about that."
  2. "Let me think out loud, so you can see where I am going."
  3. "I will start with the simple version and improve it."
  4. "Before I answer, can I write down the inputs and the outputs?"
  5. "I want to answer that properly, so give me a moment."
  6. "Can I come back to that? I want to finish this thread first."
  7. "Let me restate what we have so far."
  8. "One moment, I am choosing between two approaches."

Signpost, so short sentences still sound organised

  1. "I will cover three things: the data model, the read path, the write path."
  2. "The short answer is X. Here is the reasoning."
  3. "First ... second ... last ..." as audible markers, not moreover and furthermore.
  4. "That is the happy path. Now the failure cases."
  5. "Two options. One is X, two is Y. I would take X, because ..."
  6. "Let me park that and come back to it."
  7. "To summarise before I move on ..."
  8. "I am moving from the design to the trade-offs now."

Self-correct mid-sentence without collapsing

  1. "Let me rephrase that."
  2. "What I mean is ..."
  3. "I said delete, I meant invalidate."
  4. "Let me say that again, more simply."
  5. "Ignore that last sentence. The point is ..."
  6. "In other words ..."
  7. "Let me back up one step."
  8. "I am going to restart that sentence."

Recover when the word will not come

  1. "I do not have the English word. It is the thing that ..."
  2. "The word escapes me. The property I need is ..."
  3. "Can I draw it instead?"
  4. "You would probably call this a queue. Is that right here?"
  5. "Bear with me, I am translating."
  6. "Not a transaction. I mean writes where either all happen or none do."
  7. "Correct my terminology if it is off. The concept is ..."
  8. "Whatever it is called, the guarantee I want is ..."

The 15 terms that derail system design answers

None of these are hard words. Each sits one letter or one syllable from a different word, so a slip makes a correct design sound wrong. Say the plain version once, then use the term itself.

TermThe mix-up that costs youSay this once, first
consistentHeard as persistent. One letter, opposite claims."A read that returns the latest write."
persistentThe same collision in reverse."Data that survives a restart."
eventual consistencyTaken as "finally it works", after the false friend in Spanish, French and Portuguese."Readers may see old data briefly."
latencyMerged with throughput under "speed"."Time for one request."

synchronous / asynchronousOne unstressed syllable, gone at speed."The caller waits" and "the caller does not."
stateless / statefulNear identical spoken, and it flips your scaling story."Holds no session data" and "holds session data."

idempotentStress lands wrong and nobody catches the word."Calling it twice does what calling it once does."

One answer, rewritten for clarity rather than accent

An answer to "how would you handle the read load here". None of the changes below are pronunciation.

Before. "So basically I am thinking that maybe we can use a cache, because when the users are doing a lot of requests then the database it will be slow, and also we can put a queue in the middle so the writes are not lost, and eventually it will become consistent, but it depends of the requirements which you did not tell me."

After. "Let me start with the read path. Reads here are roughly a hundred times more frequent than writes. So I would put a cache in front of the database and serve most reads from it. Writes go straight to the database. Two questions change my answer: how stale can a read be, and is this one region or several?"

Sixty-three words became fifty-nine, so length is not the point. One idea per sentence, not five clauses joined by "and also". A number where "a lot" was. Conclusion first, reasoning second. The hedges basically and maybe are gone, because in English they signal low confidence rather than politeness. And "you did not tell me" becomes an offer, since that direct form is neutral in many languages but lands as blame here.

Why a rolling transcript changes the failure mode

Producing English is the part everyone practises. Receiving it is the part that loses questions. A constraint is stated once, at speed, through a compressed codec, in an unfamiliar accent, and the meaning sits in the small words: not, at most, read-heavy, without, duplicates. Miss one and you spend eight confident minutes solving a different problem.

Reading is easier than listening, because you set the pace and can look twice. That is the argument for having the interviewer's words on screen. OfferPilot AI's Interview Copilot takes their audio from Windows system playback, so headphones are fine, transcribes on-device with Whisper, and shows a rolling transcript about a second later. The missed word is still there while you talk, so it costs a glance, not the question.

Two rules keep it useful rather than distracting: read for the nouns and the numbers, never the grammar, and ask the interviewer before hunting through text.

Where this does not help

Worth saying plainly, since we make the thing. Whisper is good, not perfect: it mangles proper nouns, internal jargon and crosstalk, and a heavily accented interviewer is where transcription is hardest and you need it most. The text lands a second behind, so you read the previous sentence while the current one is spoken.

The overlay is excluded from screen capture at the operating-system level, so it is absent from Zoom, Teams, Meet, OBS and screenshots. It does nothing about a webcam, a lockdown browser, eye tracking, a second-device camera or a proctor in the room, and none of it is a route around a monitored assessment. Windows 10 and 11 only. Phone screens and in-person rounds are out of scope, so the phrase bank has to stand alone.
