Using an AI copilot in a product manager case interview

2026 · ~9 min read · OfferPilot AI

A drafted answer that arrives a second after the question is a structure, not an idea. In a product manager case that distinction decides everything, because all five case types are graded on the product opinion sitting inside the structure rather than on the structure itself. A copilot will reliably hand you the clarifying question, the user segments, the pain list, a metric and the risks. It will not hand you the insight about the user or the call about what you are refusing to build, and those are the two lines the interviewer writes down. Here is the map of where a skeleton helps, and one product design prompt run all the way through to the point where it runs out.

The five case types, and what one second of drafting is worth in each

PM loops recycle five shapes. They reward different things, and a generated spine is worth a different amount in each. The column that matters is the last one.

Case typePrompt it arrives asWhat a drafted spine reliably gives youWhat you still have to supply
Product design "Design a lunch ordering experience for office teams." Clarify, segment, pain, solution, metric, risk, in that order, with nothing skipped. Which segment, and the one non-obvious thing you believe about them.
Product improvement "How would you improve Google Photos?" A goal statement and a funnel stage to attach the improvement to. What the product already ships. Proposing a feature that launched two years ago ends the round on the spot, and no draft knows the current release notes.
Product strategy "Should Spotify build a ticketing marketplace?" The axes: market size, capability fit, moat, cost of entry, and a kill condition. Which single constraint the company is actually up against, which is usually distribution or licensing rather than engineering.
Metrics and root cause "Weekly actives dropped eight percent. Go." The diagnostic tree: instrumentation first, then internal changes, then external, then segment splits. The first split you choose. Platform, geography, cohort age and new versus returning are not equivalent guesses, and picking well is most of the round.
Estimation "How many delivery bikes does London need at noon?" A decomposition and an order-of-magnitude sanity check at the end. Every anchor number, defended. This is the type where a drafted answer is most dangerous, because the follow-up is always "where did that figure come from", and there is no answering that about a number you did not choose.

Read down the third column and a pattern appears. A one-second draft is good at the parts of a case that are identical every time, and blank on the parts that change with the prompt. That is not a defect in the model. It is what "generated before you have thought about it" means.

One product design prompt, run to the point where the draft stops

The prompt, phrased the way it usually lands:

Design a food delivery feature for someone ordering lunch for their whole team at the office.

A copilot listening to that returns a speakable skeleton in roughly a second. Stripped to its bones it looks like this, and it is genuinely competent:

Clarify. Are we optimising for order volume or for repeat use, and is this a new surface or an extension of the existing cart?

Users. The organiser who places the order, the colleagues who eat, the finance owner who pays, the restaurant absorbing one very large ticket.

Pick one. The organiser.

Pains. Collecting everyone's choices, splitting the payment, timing delivery to a meeting, handling a missing item across ten people.

Solutions. A shareable group cart link, per-person payment, scheduled delivery to a room, a group-level refund flow.

Prioritise. Highest impact, lowest effort first.

Metric. Repeat group orders per organiser per month.

Risks. Organisers who churn after one bad order.

Say that out loud and it runs about fifty seconds. It is organised, it covers the ground, and it is close to what every other competent candidate in that loop will say. Nothing in it can lose you the round. Nothing in it can win the round either, because "prioritise by impact and effort" is a promise to prioritise rather than an act of prioritising.

The user insight, which had to come from you

The organiser is not a coordinator. The organiser is an underwriter. Their card is the one on file, they front the money, and they personally absorb every failure in the chain: the missing side, the cold noodles, the colleague who says they will transfer the money and then does not. So the product's real failure event is not a bad meal. It is an organiser who quietly stops volunteering, and because a team has roughly one organiser, losing that person removes ten diners in a single stroke.

That reframe changes the metric the skeleton offered. Repeat orders per organiser per month is a slow decay curve. The thing to instrument is organiser survival after their first failed order, because the churn here is an incident rather than a trend, and an incident has a timestamp you can hang a recovery flow on.

The prioritisation call, which is the part with a price

Given that insight, here is the call: cut the shareable group cart link, and build per-person pre-authorised payment plus an incident flow that refunds the organiser first and reclaims from the platform rather than from a colleague.

Then state what it costs, out loud, because a prioritisation call with no cost attached is not a call:

  • The group cart link demos beautifully, ships in about a sprint, and is what the team already wants to build. I am taking it off the roadmap.
  • Per-person pre-auth needs payment work in every market and adds a step at checkout, so first-time group order conversion will visibly drop in month one. I am accepting that drop.
  • I would make the trade because an order I lose is one order, and an organiser I lose is a team. I would check it at sixty days, and if organiser survival after a failed order has not moved, the call was wrong and I would say so.

No model produced those three lines. They came from having been the person holding the receipt. An insight, in this round, means a claim about the user that was not in the prompt, is not true of everyone, and changes what gets built. A prioritisation call means naming the thing you are not building and the price you are paying for that. A skeleton contains neither, by construction, because it is the shape of an answer generated before the answer exists.

How to use a spine without sounding like you are reading one

Ask your clarifying question before you look at anything

Speak first. One question, chosen by you, buys twenty to thirty seconds of interviewer talking time and sets the frame. If your opening move is silence while you read, the round starts with a pause you then have to explain.

Take the headings, leave the sentences

Glance at the section labels only, then rebuild each one in your own words. Prose read verbatim has a flatness that experienced interviewers pick up within two sentences, and a PM round is scored partly on how you communicate.

Interrupt the spine at "solutions" and put your insight in

Say the words "the thing I actually believe about this user is", and then say it. That sentence is the pivot from a generic case walk to an answer with a person behind it, and it has to be yours.

Announce the cut before they ask for it

Name what you are not building and what it costs. Volunteering a trade-off unprompted is the highest-signal move available in a design case, and it is the one thing no draft can do for you, because it does not know what you are willing to lose.

Where this is the wrong tool, honestly

Of every interview format, the PM case round is the least favourable one for live assistance. It is a dialogue, not a monologue. A good interviewer interrupts inside your first thirty seconds, pushes on the metric, and doubles back to a segment you dismissed. There is no editor to paste into and nothing to compile. And in a design or strategy round at an onsite you may be sketching on a whiteboard or a shared board, or sitting in a room with the interviewer, in which case a desktop overlay is not in the conversation at all.

What to rehearse instead

Practise supplying the two missing pieces, since the skeleton is the part you will always have. Take ten prompts, and for each one write a single sentence of user insight and a single sentence naming what you would cut and what that costs. Two sentences per prompt, twenty sentences in total, spoken aloud against a timer. The drill is uncomfortable in a way that reading frameworks is not, which is the sign that it is the useful one.

Vary the prompts by business model rather than by industry, because the model is what changes the answer: marketplace, subscription, ads, enterprise seats, usage-based.
