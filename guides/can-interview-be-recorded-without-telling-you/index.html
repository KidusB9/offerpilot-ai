Can a company record your interview without telling you?

2026 · ~7 min read · OfferPilot AI

In most of the United States, yes. Federal wiretap law and roughly two thirds of states run on one-party consent, and the interviewer is a party to the conversation, so their consent alone is enough to make the recording lawful. About a dozen states require everybody on the call to agree. The practical rule is simpler than the legal one: assume you are being recorded, because the disclosure you were given was probably a line of text in a calendar invite you accepted without reading.

One-party and all-party, in plain terms

Wiretap statutes ask a single question: how many people in the conversation have to know. Under the federal standard, one is enough, and that one can be the person doing the recording. An interviewer taping their own interview is not eavesdropping on anybody. They are recording a conversation they are in.

An all-party state flips that. Everyone whose voice is captured must consent, and in several of those states a violation is a criminal matter rather than a polite disagreement. Consent does not have to be signed. In practice it is implied: you are told at the top of the call, you keep talking, that is consent. This is exactly why the announcement exists. It is not courtesy, it is the mechanism that makes the recording legal.

Two complications matter to a candidate more than the headline rule. First, remote interviews cross state lines constantly, and which state's law governs a call between a recruiter in Texas and a candidate in California is genuinely unsettled. Employers with counsel resolve it by defaulting to the strictest reading, which is why national companies disclose everywhere. Second, these statutes are older than video platforms, so they reach Zoom and Teams by analogy rather than by name.

The states usually listed as all-party

Treat this as a map of where to be careful, not as legal advice. Published counts disagree with each other because several states are ambiguous on their face and have been narrowed or widened by courts.

GroupStatesWhat makes it messy
Commonly listed as all-partyCalifornia, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, WashingtonThe clearest group. Several attach criminal penalties. Washington accepts an announcement recorded at the start of the call as the consent itself
All-party with a twistMassachusetts, OregonMassachusetts bans secret recording rather than unconsented recording, so a disclosed one is fine. Oregon has historically treated in-person conversations more strictly than phone calls

Everywhere elseThe remaining majorityOne-party consent. The interviewer can record without telling you and without breaking the law

None of this decides whether the employer should tell you, only whether a prosecutor could act. Data protection law is a separate track. In the UK and the EU, recording a candidate needs a lawful basis and clear notice under the GDPR regardless of any wiretap question, and that transparency duty is stronger than anything in US state law.

Why most interviewers disclose twice

You will often get the same information in two forms, and it is worth knowing what each one is doing.

  • Written, before the call. A sentence in the invitation, a checkbox on the application, or the terms of an assessment platform. This is the paper trail. It exists so the company can show a date-stamped notice that predates the conversation.
  • Spoken or on-screen, at the start. The interviewer says it, or the platform posts a banner and a consent prompt. This catches everybody the paperwork misses: anyone who joined late, dialed in by phone, was added by a colleague, or never opened the invite.

The second one is what creates implied consent under an all-party statute, because it is the only one you demonstrably heard. If you were told once in writing and never out loud, that is unremarkable in a one-party state and thinner cover in an all-party one.

How to check, in the first twenty seconds

Look for the platform's own indicator

Major meeting platforms mark an active recording in the window itself, generally a red dot or badge near the top and often a one-time consent dialog offering continue or leave. Exact placement moves between versions and hosts can configure some of it, so scan the whole window rather than one corner.

Read the participant list for a bot

Standalone notetakers usually join as a named participant, typically a product name followed by the word Notetaker. Assistants built into the platform run inside the host's client instead, so they may surface as a banner or a small icon rather than as a person. Either way, a transcript outlives the call.

Re-open the invite

In a one-party state this is where the disclosure usually lives, sitting under the join link. It also names the platform, which tells you whether you are in an ordinary meeting or a vendor assessment tool where the recording is the product being sold to the employer.

Ask, once, in one sentence

Say it in the first minute while it is still small talk: Before we start, is this call being recorded? Asking early reads as organised. Asking at minute thirty reads as though something just went wrong. You get a straight answer either way, and then you know what you are speaking into.

What to say, and what each ask actually gets you

Wording matters less than timing, but here is what to expect from each version.

What you sayLikely responseWhat it really gets you
Is this call being recorded?Almost always a direct yes or noCertainty, plus a note in your own file about who told you what. Costs nothing socially
I would rather not be recorded, would that be alright?Often yes in a normal conversational round, since stopping is one click and notes are the fallbackThe safest phrasing. It offers an out instead of making a demand, so a no is easy for them to explain and easy for you to accept
Who sees it, and how long is it kept?Usually a partial answer, sometimes a handover to HRMore than you expect. Recordings often reach a whole hiring panel and sit in the applicant tracking system long after the decision
Could I have a copy afterwards?Usually no in the US, more traction under UK and EU data protection lawLittle in the moment, but it puts the request on the record before a decision, which is the only point at which it looks routine
Nothing at allNothingA recording you may never see, made under rules you never checked. Fine most of the time, which is why most people choose it

Two things not to do. Do not open with the legal argument. Citing a statute in minute one turns the interview into a negotiation, and the recruiter's honest reaction is to wonder what you are planning to say. And do not start your own secret recording as a counter-move. In an all-party state that is the same conduct you were worried about, pointed the other way, and the person with less power in the room is the one carrying the risk.

Getting a copy after the fact

Ask the recruiter by email first, briefly, and keep the tone administrative. Employers say no more often than yes, because interview footage is treated as internal hiring documentation alongside scorecards and notes.

Where you have a real lever, it is data protection law rather than employment law. Under the UK GDPR and its EU equivalent, a subject access request obliges the employer to give you a copy of the personal data they hold about you, usually within one month, with other people's information redacted. Some interviewer opinions and assessment material can fall under exemptions, so a request does not always return a clean video file. In California, applicants have been inside the scope of the state privacy statute since the start of 2023, which creates rights to know and to delete that are worth reading if you interview there. Illinois has a separate rule for video interviews analysed by AI, including a right to have the video destroyed on request. Retention elsewhere is set by the employer's own policy and by anti-discrimination record-keeping duties, so the answer changes with the jurisdiction. Where the stakes are real, get advice rather than trusting a page like this one.

Where a copilot is the wrong tool for this problem

Worth saying plainly, because people arrive at this question from two very different places. If you are asking because you want to be composed on a call that is being recorded, live help is useful. If you are asking because you believe something unlawful was said to you in an interview, software is not the answer, and a secret recording of your own may be the worst move available.

  • It is not an evidence trail. Interview Copilot exists to help you answer in the moment, not to archive a conversation for later. Do not build a legal plan on it.
  • Capture exclusion is narrower than it sounds. The overlay is excluded from screen capture at the operating-system level, so it does not appear in Zoom, Teams, Meet, OBS or screenshots. That says nothing about a webcam pointed at your face, a lockdown browser, an invigilator in the room, eye tracking, or a phone propped up as a second camera. Different channel, different rules.
  • Windows only. Windows 10 and 11, no macOS, no Linux, no mobile, no browser extension. If the interview is on a work laptop you do not control, the question is moot.
  • It cannot tell you which state's law applies. Nothing here is legal advice, and the interstate question above is unsettled enough that lawyers argue about it in court.

The rule to carry into the call

Assume the recording exists. Assume a transcript exists too, because the transcript is what gets searched, quoted in a debrief, and pasted into a hiring decision. If you are in an all-party state and nobody announced anything, you have a fair question to ask, and asking it early costs you nothing. If you want it stopped, offer the out rather than making the demand. If you want a copy, ask in writing before the decision lands, and know that outside the UK and EU the answer is usually no. Then spend the rest of your attention on the part of the interview you actually control, which is what the transcript ends up saying.
