Pramp alternative: where free peer mock interviews stop working

2026 · ~7 min read · OfferPilot AI

Pramp is free, peer to peer, and genuinely good at one thing: making you talk through a problem to a stranger on a clock. It stops working at four specific points. Your partner is a candidate too, so they cannot tell you where the real hiring bar sits. Slots get ghosted, and a rematch is not the session you booked. The feedback form pulls people towards commenting on how you explained things rather than on whether your answer was correct. And the question pool is generic by design, so you never rehearse what your named company actually asks. The move is not to replace Pramp. It is to stop asking it for the three jobs it structurally cannot do.

What an hour of peer matching actually consists of

Peer platforms of this shape run a fixed structure, and knowing the structure is what tells you where the gaps are. A session is one hour, split down the middle. You sit in the candidate seat for one half and the interviewer seat for the other. Whoever is interviewing gets a prompt page: the question, a worked solution, hints laid out in the order they should be released, and follow-up questions for a candidate who finishes early. There is a shared editor and a video call. At the end, both sides fill in a short feedback form with ratings across a few dimensions plus a free-text box.

Pramp has been free at the point of use, with the cost paid in your own time rather than money, and it is reported to now sit under Exponent. Both are reported arrangements checked on 2026-08-31 rather than quotes, so confirm them yourself before building a schedule around them.

Read that structure again and the four failure modes fall straight out of it. They are not bugs. They are what free peer matching is.

The four things free peer matching cannot do

1. Your peer cannot calibrate the real bar

The interviewer seat comes with a solution sheet, so "correct" collapses into "matched the sheet". That tells you whether you arrived at one written answer. It does not tell you whether a hiring committee would have passed you. Real interviewers weigh things that never appear on a prompt page: did you state the complexity before being asked, did you notice the input could be empty, did you keep narrating while you typed, was your first idea reasonable or did it take three hints to get you moving. Your partner has never had to write a hire recommendation and defend it in a debrief. Someone a week into their prep will call a mediocre answer strong, and someone far ahead of you is still reading from the same sheet you would have read from. Their verdict is worth roughly what it costs.

2. People do not turn up

You book an early slot, block the hour, get up for it, and nobody is there. Platforms of this kind generally try to rematch you with whoever else is free at that moment, and some offer a solo fallback instead, but a rematch is a different question at a different level with a partner you did not choose. The cost is not the lost hour alone, it is the calendar discipline you spent protecting it. Budget for a share of booked sessions to evaporate, and never make a peer session the only preparation on a given day. If it is the only thing on the list and it dissolves, the day is gone.

3. The feedback lands on delivery, not correctness

Look at what people actually write in that free-text box. Clear communicator. Explained the approach well. Seemed nervous at the start, settled after ten minutes. That is not laziness, it is the honest limit of what an untrained observer can see. Delivery is observable without expertise. Correctness is not. Whether your O(n log n) was actually achievable in O(n), whether your recursion would blow the stack at n of ten to the fifth, whether the schema you waved at survives the write pattern, all of that needs someone who has solved that class of problem more often than you have. Delivery feedback is useful. It is just not the feedback that fails candidates.

4. The questions are generic because they have to be

One pool serves everybody, so it converges on the middle: an array manipulation, a graph traversal, tell me about a conflict with a coworker. Your loop is not the middle. Companies run rounds that no general pool contains, such as a bar raiser style round graded against a published principle list, a written design document round, a debugging exercise on an unfamiliar codebase, or a product sense round for a data role. You can put forty hours through a peer pool and still walk into a round whose shape you have never seen. A bank is what closes that gap: the company Q&A generator takes a named company and role plus your own resume and builds questions shaped by both, and the interview questions library covers the general ground for free.

A week that uses peer reps for stamina and a copilot for the real loop

Split the jobs instead of hunting for one tool that does all of them. Peer sessions are stamina training. Solo work is where skill is actually built. Live assistance belongs in the rounds that count, and it teaches you nothing between them.

Two peer sessions a week, booked at interview o'clock

If your onsites land at 10am, book 10am. The point is not the question, it is holding a coherent thought out loud for forty-five minutes with a stranger watching. That capacity is trainable and it decays. Two a week keeps it, and it survives one of them being ghosted.

Take the interviewer seat seriously

This is the most undervalued half hour on the platform. Sitting with a solution sheet, deciding when to release a hint, watching someone sail past the obvious edge case, is the only free look you get at the problem from the other side of the table. Most people treat that half as the toll they pay for the half they wanted. Treat it as the lesson.

Do the correctness work alone, within the hour

Your peer told you that you communicated well. Nobody told you whether the answer was right. So go back to the problem while the discomfort is fresh and settle it yourself: the optimal approach, why it is optimal, the complexity, and the case you missed. This is the part that moves you, and it happens with nobody watching.

Switch to the company bank once a loop is real

General drilling is for the months when nothing is scheduled. The week a real onsite appears, stop feeding the generic pool and rehearse the named rounds instead, using the company's own published process notes where they exist. Most careers pages name the rounds, and some name the rubric.

Use live assistance in the round, not in the mock

In a peer session there is nobody to hide from and nothing to gain from hiding, because the unassisted rep is the entire value. Save it for the interview itself, where the failure mode is a blank thirty seconds on a question you half know. The copilot listens to the interviewer's audio through system playback, transcribes on device, and drafts a speakable answer grounded in your resume and that company bank in roughly a second.

The honest case against leaning on a copilot

Say it outright: a copilot teaches you nothing between sessions. The draft appears, you speak, the round ends, and there is no residue. You cannot revise from it, you did not encode anything, and the next question starts from the same baseline as the last one. Two candidates who look identical in a live round diverge the moment a follow-up goes off script, and the one who did the unassisted reps is the one still standing. If you skip the peer sessions because assistance feels like a substitute for them, you have swapped a skill for a crutch, and a full loop will find that out.

Three more limits worth naming. It is Windows 10 and 11 only, with no macOS build, no Linux, no mobile app and no browser extension, so on a Mac the peer half of this plan is open to you and the copilot half is not. The overlay is excluded from screen capture at the operating system level, which keeps it out of Zoom, Teams, Meet, OBS and screenshots, and that is the whole of what that does: it has no effect on a webcam, a lockdown browser, eye tracking, a second-device camera, or a person watching you in the room. And it does not belong in a proctored assessment, where the rules of the sitting are the rules regardless of what any tool can technically do.

There is also a case where none of this is your problem. If you can already solve the problems and what breaks is your voice, your pace or your filler words, no copilot measures any of that, because it listens to the interviewer rather than to you. A recording tool that scores your own speech is the right purchase there, and peer sessions are already the cheapest version of one.

What to actually do this week

Book two peer sessions and protect the slots. Take the interviewer seat properly in both. Spend the hour after each one settling the correctness question your partner could not answer. When a real onsite lands on the calendar, stop drilling the general pool and build the bank for that company from your resume with the Q&A generator, then decide separately whether you want live assistance in the round itself. The Windows download is free for five minutes of listening a day with no card, which is enough to find out whether the second half of that plan is for you at all.
