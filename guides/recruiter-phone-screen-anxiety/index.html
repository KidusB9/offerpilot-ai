Recruiter phone screen anxiety: the crib sheet and eight answers

2026 · ~9 min read · OfferPilot AI

Phone screen anxiety is mostly a memory problem wearing a fear costume. You are being asked about eight predictable questions with no face to read, and you are allowed to have notes in front of you that nobody can see. So the fix is one page of text where your eyes already land, plus two rehearsed phrases that buy you four seconds each. Below is the layout of that page, the eight answers word for word including the salary deflection, and what to do when your voice goes.

Why the phone version feels worse than the video version

Three things are missing, and each of them is normally doing work for you. There is no nodding, so you get no signal that the answer landed. There is keyboard noise, which sounds like judgement but is a recruiter filling in a form field. And silence is ambiguous: on video a pause reads as thinking, on a phone line a pause past roughly four seconds reads as a dropped call, so you rush to fill it and talk yourself into a corner.

None of that is fixed by calming down. It is fixed by removing everything you would otherwise be retrieving from memory while your pulse is at 110.

The one page crib sheet, block by block

One page, landscape, 18pt or larger so you can read it at a glance rather than leaning in. Print it if you can, because a second window means alt tabbing and alt tabbing has a sound. Five blocks in fixed positions, so your eye knows where to go without searching for it.

One rule holds the whole thing together: nothing on the page is a complete sentence except the salary lines and the stall phrases. Everything else is a fragment. Fragments prompt you. Sentences get performed.

Buying thinking time

You need three to five seconds, roughly twice, and you need it to sound like consideration rather than a freeze. The trick is that every pause has to carry a sound, otherwise the recruiter starts wondering whether the line dropped. These are the four that work, ordered by how much time they buy.

  • "Let me write that down." Four to six seconds, and it is the only one that survives being used twice. It is also true, so write something.
  • "Can I take that in two parts?" Three seconds, and it quietly gives you permission to answer the half you are ready for first.
  • "I would rather give you the accurate version than the quick one." Three seconds, plus it sets an expectation that the answer will be considered.
  • Repeat the question back as a narrowing. "So you mean the migration specifically, not the whole platform." Two seconds, and it often gets you a clarification you actually needed.

A sip of water buys two more seconds and nobody hears it as a stall. What does not work: "that is a great question", which is audibly a stall, and "um, so, basically", which is three words of nothing at the exact moment your voice is being assessed.

The eight answers, word for word

Fill the brackets, say each one out loud once against a stopwatch, then reduce it to seven words on the crib sheet.

1. Walk me through your background

I am a [backend engineer], [six] years, mostly in [payments]. Right now at [company] I own [the settlement service]. The piece closest to this role is [the migration off the legacy processor], which I [led across two quarters]. I am looking at [platform] roles now, which is why I picked this one up.

Forty seconds. Three beats: what you are, what you own, the one thing that matches this posting. Then stop talking. The recruiter is checking that your summary matches your CV, not running an interview yet.

2. Why are you looking?

I have been at [company] [three] years and I got [the platform migration] done. What I cannot get there is [ownership of a service end to end]. That is the reason, rather than anything going wrong.

One negative clause, maximum. The last sentence exists to close the follow up before it is asked.

3. What do you know about us?

You do [product] for [who]. The posting says [phrase lifted from the description], and that is the part I applied for, because [one sentence about your own work]. What I do not know is [genuine question about the team or the roadmap], which I was going to ask you.

Naming one gap and converting it into a question is stronger than a rehearsed summary of the About page, and it is the only version of this answer that cannot be caught out.

4. What are your salary expectations?

Three asks, three different answers. Do not skip to the third.

Build X and Y before the call, not during it. Y is the number you would accept without thinking about it. X sits roughly ten percent under Y and comfortably above your walk away figure, which never gets said out loud at any point in the process. If a range was posted, do not open below it, because you cannot be paid less than public information for being polite.

5. What is your notice period?

[Four] weeks written, and I would give notice the day I sign. That puts me at [a date already on your crib sheet].

"Fairly quickly, I think" is the answer of somebody who has not checked their contract, and the recruiter has to type a real date into a field.

6. What else do you have in process?

Two other processes, one at final stage with a decision expected around [date]. Not trying to create pressure, that is just the timeline I am working with.

If there is nothing else, say "this is the one I have moved on so far" and move on yourself. Never invent a competing offer. The follow up is which company, and the hesitation before you answer that costs you more than having no offer did.

7. How many years of [X]? Rate yourself one to ten

[Four] years, [the last two of them in production on the payments service].

This is a form field, so give the number first and one clause of evidence after it. For the one to ten version, an eight with a boundary beats a ten: "eight, strong on [X], I would want a week with [Y]." A ten schedules an ambush with the hiring manager. For a technology you have genuinely not used, say so and give the nearest neighbour in one sentence. "I am a fast learner" is the phrase that gets you screened out.

8. Do you have any questions for me?

Ask things this specific person can answer. A recruiter usually knows the process, the headcount and the band, and usually does not know the architecture.

  • "What does the loop after this look like, how many stages and what is in each?"
  • "Is this new headcount or a backfill?"
  • "What band would you be putting me at, and what is the range on it?"

Then close with: "Anything from this call you would want me to tighten before the next stage?" Recruiters answer that honestly far more often than you would expect, and it is free coaching from the person who just took the notes.

The first ninety seconds

Stand up before you answer

On an audio call you can, and nobody knows. Standing changes your breath support, which is the thing that actually shakes, and it stops the slump that makes you sound flat.

Answer on the second ring

The first ring is where you exhale fully. A long exhale before the first word does more for a shaking voice than any amount of telling yourself to relax.

Spend the admin thirty seconds

Confirm you can hear them, use their first name once, then let them run their intro. You are not wasting time, you are getting your voice warm on sentences that carry no risk.

Recover out loud if you lose the thread

"Sorry, I have gone off the point. The short version is [thing]." Nobody marks you down for that. What gets written down is whether you recovered or kept going.

Two practical things beforehand: silence notifications, because one message tone takes a whole sentence with it, and put the water within arm reach rather than in the kitchen. Where a live copilot is the wrong tool for this call

Worth being blunt, since a vendor is publishing this page. A real phone screen is a call to your phone. OfferPilot AI and its Interview Copilot listen to system playback audio on a Windows PC, so if a recruiter rings your mobile and you hold it to your ear, the copilot hears nothing at all. There is no mobile build and no way to bridge a cellular call into it. It only applies when the screen runs through the computer, on Zoom, Teams or Meet, and it is Windows 10 and 11 only, so there is nothing to try on a Mac.

Even on a desktop call, this particular conversation is a poor fit. A recruiter screen moves fast and turns on facts no model can know: your notice period, your number, why you are leaving. Reading a drafted answer is slower than glancing at a fragment on paper, and it sounds slower too. The overlay being excluded from screen capture, which is what keeps it out of a Zoom or Teams share, is simply irrelevant on an audio call, and it has never done anything about a webcam, a lockdown browser, or a person sitting in the room. The honest use here is preparation before the call, with the copilot itself earning its place in the technical rounds that come after this one.

Rehearse the follow ups, not the scripts

Read each answer aloud once, cut anything that is not one of its beats, then reduce it to seven words on the page. Say the salary lines three times, because that is the one you will fumble under pressure and the only one with money attached to the fumble. Then put the page down and practise the recovery: get deliberately lost in an answer and pull yourself back with the sentence in the bottom strip.
