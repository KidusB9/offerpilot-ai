The negotiation call: what to say at five specific moments

2026 · ~7 min read · OfferPilot AI

Do not counter on the call where you first hear the number. Take down the whole package, name the day you will come back, and hang up. The counter belongs on a second call, where you say one number, tie the gap to something specific about the job, and attach a signing condition. Below are the lines for the five moments where the figure actually moves, in the order they happen.

Why this is two calls and not one

The recruiter's offer call has a script of its own: deliver the number, gauge your reaction, get a soft yes. Everything you say in the ninety seconds after hearing a figure is unpriced, because you have not compared it to anything and you have not read the vesting schedule yet. Reacting warmly is the expensive mistake. A recruiter who writes down candidate sounded delighted has no reason to walk down the hall and ask for more. Reacting badly is worse, because it turns a routine approval into a conversation about whether you are actually going to accept at all.

So the first call is collection. The second call is negotiation. Splitting them costs you two days and buys you the only thing that matters, which is a number you chose in advance rather than one you improvised with your heart rate up.

Moment 1: they say the offer out loud

The pause after the number is a question. Fill it with clerical work, not with an opinion.

“Thank you, I appreciate you moving quickly on this. Before I say anything else, can I take the whole thing down so I am not working from memory? Base, the bonus target and how it is measured, the equity figure and the vesting schedule, any sign-on, and the start date you have in mind. And what date do you need an answer by?”

Write the answers as they are said, including the awkward ones. Whether the bonus is company funded or individual. Whether the equity number is a dollar value or a share count, and at which price. Whether a sign-on carries a repayment clause if you leave inside a year, which is common and rarely volunteered. Then close the call yourself:

“That is really helpful. I want to read the equity schedule properly rather than react to it now, so I would rather come back to you with something specific. Can I call you Thursday morning?”

Naming a day is the whole trick. “I need to think about it” with no date attached produces a pressure call on Friday afternoon. A named morning reads as a plan.

The 48 hours in between, step by step

Figures below are illustrative. Substitute your own market and currency.

Write the offer out in four lines, not one total

Base 185,000. Bonus target 10 percent, marked separately because it is contingent. Equity 240,000 over four years with a one-year cliff, which is 60,000 a year if the valuation holds. Sign-on 15,000, minus tax, possibly repayable. A single blended total-comp figure hides which line you can actually move.

Pick one number, not a range

Ranges belong to the screening call. At offer stage a range is read as its bottom, every time. Decide the single base figure that makes you sign without hesitating. Say 205,000. That is the number you will speak on Thursday.

Source the gap to the job, in one sentence

Not to your rent, not to a website average. Something said during the loop: the role owns the payments service, it carries the on-call rotation, the level came out higher than the posting described. This is the sentence that makes your number approvable by someone who was never on the call.

Rank two fallback currencies and write your walk-away

If base will not move, what do you take instead, and in what order? Sign-on, extra equity, start date, remote days. Decide now. The recruiter will ask, and a pause at that point reads as improvisation and invites a smaller answer.

Moment 2: you counter

Four parts, in this order, then silence.

“I want to work here. The loop confirmed it and I am ready to sign. On base, I was targeting 205 and the offer is at 185. The gap for me is scope: this role owns the payments service and carries the on-call rotation, which is more than the posting described. If we can get base to 205, I will sign today.”

Enthusiasm first kills the question the recruiter is silently asking, which is whether you are shopping the offer. One number gives them something an approver can say yes to. The sourced gap gives them the sentence to say when they get there. The signing condition turns your request from a wish into a trade. Then stop talking, because whatever the recruiter says next tells you where the flexibility actually lives.

Three lines to keep out of your mouth. “I was hoping for a bit more” contains no number, so nobody can approve it. “Market rate for this role is” invites a request for your source, and if the source is a crowd-sourced average you have lost the frame you were standing on. And do not claim a competing offer you do not have, because the follow-up is which company and by what date, and that conversation ends badly.

Moment 3: they say the band is fixed

Usually this is true, and usually the recruiter did not set the band. Fighting it wastes the one call you get. Accept the constraint out loud, then move to a different budget line.

“That is fair, and I am not asking anyone to break a band. Two questions, then. Is the band fixed for this level, or is the level itself still open? And what is in the package outside base, is there a sign-on we could use to close the 20?”

The second question is the practical one. At many companies base sits inside a level band signed off by a compensation function, while a one-time sign-on comes from a different pot with a shorter approval path. The first question is the bigger prize and the slower one: a level change generally means going back to a committee or a hiring manager, and it can add a week or more to your timeline. Ask it anyway, but ask it knowing what it costs in calendar days.

Moment 4: “what would it take for you to sign today?”

This is a closing question, not an invitation to list wishes. Answer in one sentence, with at most two items, and name only things you will genuinely honour.

“Base at 205 and a start date of the 6th. Everything else in the offer works as written, and if you can get those two I will sign today.”

If you are not ready to commit, say that rather than inventing a condition:

“I do not want to give you a number and then move off it. I have one conversation to finish this week. If base lands at 205, that conversation gets very short.”

The trap here is the one that quietly damages offers. You name a condition, they go and get it approved, and then you ask for one more thing. That single move spends the goodwill you need later for your start date, for how the sign-on is paid out, and for whoever speaks up about you at your first review. Ask once, ask clearly, then honour what you said.

Moment 5: you accept, or you close the loop

Accepting should be unambiguous, and it should end in writing.

“That works. 205 base, 15 sign-on, start on the 6th, and I am accepting. Send the paperwork whenever it is ready and I will sign it the same day. Could you put those numbers in an email so we are both working from the same sheet?”

Declining is worth doing properly, because recruiters move companies and they remember who was straightforward with them.

“I am going to pass, and I want to be straight about why. It came down to base against another commitment, not the team. I would happily take a call from you again.”

And then the moment nobody writes a script for: they said they would check on something, and went quiet. Do not wait it out and do not send a third apologetic nudge.

“You mentioned you would come back on the sign-on. Where did that land? I have a decision to make by Friday and I would rather make it with your number than without it.”

Whatever was agreed by voice, repeat back by email the same day: base, bonus target, equity figure and schedule, sign-on and any repayment terms, start date. Not because anyone is being dishonest, but because two people remember a phone call differently and the written version is the one that reaches the person drafting your contract.

Where a live copilot is the wrong tool

Straight answer: OfferPilot AI is not a negotiation tool, and this is the page where saying so is more useful than selling. It listens to the interviewer through your computer's playback loopback, which means it works on a Zoom, Teams or Meet call and hears nothing at all if the recruiter rings your mobile, which is how a great many offer calls arrive. It is Windows 10 and 11 only. The overlay is excluded from screen capture at the operating system level, so it stays out of a shared screen or a recording, but that does nothing about a webcam or anyone sitting in the room with you, and on an offer call it is beside the point anyway.

More to the point, phrasing is not the bottleneck on this call. Arithmetic is, and so is a decision only you can make about your walk-away number. No model knows your notice period, your unvested equity, or which regret you would rather live with. Where a copilot does earn its place is the rounds before this call, the ones that decide which level you are offered at, and the level decides the band you are now trying to move inside.
