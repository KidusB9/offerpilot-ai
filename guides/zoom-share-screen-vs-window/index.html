Zoom share screen vs share window: what the other side actually sees

2026 · ~8 min read · OfferPilot AI

Share Entire Screen and the viewer gets everything drawn on that display: notification toasts with the sender's name, the taskbar, the Alt-Tab switcher, any window you drag over. Share a Window and they get that one app's pixels and nothing else, so you can Alt-Tab, take a Slack toast, or bury it under three other apps and their stream never changes. Portion of Screen streams a fixed rectangle of your desktop, whatever sits inside it. Eight behaviours, side by side, below.

Eight behaviours, three share modes

Every cell describes what the viewer's stream renders, not what your own desktop shows. Those two diverge more than people expect.

One clean summary: Entire Screen captures a display, Window captures a window, Portion captures a rectangle. Almost every unpleasant surprise comes from assuming the middle one behaves like the other two.

Why sharing a single window leaks far less than people assume

The modern Windows capture path hands an app a window's own composited output, not a cropped photograph of the screen. The compositor draws each window into its own surface, so notifications, the taskbar and the app on top never reach the buffer Zoom reads. Hence row four: your screen shows the window buried, the viewer's stream shows it clean.

Three caveats worth knowing.

  • Minimising is not the same as covering. A covered window keeps producing frames. A minimised one generally stops, leaving the viewer on a frozen frame that looks exactly like your machine has hung.
  • Child windows can go missing. Drop-down menus, combo boxes and autocomplete popups are often separate top-level windows spilling outside the shared window's bounds. You see the menu open, the viewer sees nothing, then a value changes out of nowhere. It is the usual source of "wait, what did you just click?" in a shared IDE.
  • A browser window is not a browser tab. Zoom lists each Chrome or Edge window separately, never each tab. Sharing a browser window shares every tab in it, and switching tabs is fully visible. A tab you want private must live in a different window.

In a shared-editor round that is the difference between a clean share and an accidental tour of your inbox, and it keeps the stream steady while you work the problem elsewhere with Code Copilot.

Notifications are the row that actually catches people out

Every other row is recoverable with an apology. A toast reading "Recruiter at the other company: still free Thursday?" is not. Two settings, before the call rather than during it.

  • Zoom's own switch. The desktop client's Share Screen settings include an option to silence system notifications while you share your desktop. Turn it on. Wording shifts between releases, so look for the notification line in that pane, not one exact label.
  • Windows Do Not Disturb, set by hand. Windows has an automatic focus rule for duplicating your display, and it does not fire for Zoom sharing, because sharing is not duplication. Toggle Do Not Disturb yourself from the notification centre before you join.

Where a capture-excluded overlay fits, and what it cannot do

The last row works differently. Windows lets a window be marked so the compositor strips it from any capture before an application receives the frame. OfferPilot AI uses that, so its overlay is absent from all three Zoom modes, and from Teams, Meet, OBS and a plain PrintScreen, while staying visible on your own monitor. Not a blur, not a black box: the pixels are not in the frame that gets encoded and sent. It is on every plan, free tier included, so the 60 second routine tests it before you rely on it.

The boundary matters as much as the capability. Capture exclusion covers screen sharing, recording and screenshots. It does nothing about a webcam, a lockdown browser that owns the machine, a proctor in the room, eye tracking, or a phone propped up as a second camera. Eyes reading something off to the side are visible on video, and a four second pause before every answer reads as a pause whatever caused it.

Where this guide runs out

  • It describes the Windows desktop client. A browser, macOS, a virtual machine or remote desktop routes capture through a different pipeline, and the Window column especially can differ there.
  • Clients change. Occlusion behaviour for window sharing has flipped before as capture APIs moved on. Re-run the test after a major update.
  • Some rounds make all of this irrelevant. In a proctored assessment inside a lockdown browser, the environment controls the machine and share-mode nuance is beside the point. OfferPilot AI is the wrong tool there, and we would rather say so than sell you something that does not apply. It is Windows 10 and 11 only, built for live calls and preparation, not exams.

For an ordinary Zoom interview the takeaway is small. Share a window rather than a screen whenever the interviewer does not actually need your whole desktop, silence notifications anyway, and spend one minute proving it on your phone.
