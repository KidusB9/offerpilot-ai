ADHD interview accommodations: nine asks and the exact wording

2026 · ~6 min read · OfferPilot AI

Ask the recruiting coordinator, in writing, at the moment you are being scheduled, and describe the barrier rather than the diagnosis. "I take in questions more accurately when I can read them as well as hear them, so could each question be pasted into the chat as it is asked?" is a complete request with no label attached. The four granted fastest are questions in writing, a scheduled break, one interviewer at a time, and a longer window on the take-home. Each of the nine below comes with the sentence to send, what usually happens to it, and what to prepare if the answer is no.

This is wording help, not legal advice. The ADA calls these reasonable accommodations, the Equality Act 2010 calls them reasonable adjustments, and both weigh the barrier and the change rather than the name of a condition. Employers can sometimes ask for supporting information, more often for the job itself than for an interview format. What applies to you depends on where you are and on the employer.

Send it to the right person, at the right moment

The coordinator, not the hiring manager

The coordinator owns the calendar, the room and the assessment invite. A hiring manager forwards it to them anyway and adds a day. Ask first: "Is there an accommodations contact for this process?"

At scheduling, never inside forty-eight hours

Not etiquette. Assessment platforms commonly apply an extended-time setting when your invite is generated, so a late request can mean reissuing it. Format changes to a live call are easy late. Timer changes are not.

Two asks, mechanics only

Use the word adjustment or accommodation once so it routes correctly, then go straight to logistics. Five asks in one email reads as a negotiation and slows everything down.

Re-confirm at 24 hours

The commonest failure is not refusal, it is a granted adjustment that never reaches the panel. Reply the day before: "Just confirming the five-minute break after the second session."

The nine asks, the sentence, and the fallback

Column two is what you send or say, with no diagnosis in it. Column three is generalised from how hiring processes are structured, not from any measured figure, so read it as a guide to where pushing is worth it. Column four is for when the answer is no, or is silence.

The askSay exactly thisGranted or stalledIf it is no, prep this
1. Questions in writing"I take in questions more accurately when I can read them as well as hear them. Could each question be pasted into the chat as it is asked?"Usually granted for behavioural rounds. Stalls when it sounds like "send me the questions in advance", which interviewers hear as a fairness problem.Repeat the question back first: "So, a time I disagreed with a manager, yes?" An ordinary clarifying move that puts the question into your own notes in your own words.
2. One interviewer at a time"I follow a conversation better with one speaker at a time. Could the panel run as sequential one-to-ones, even if each is shorter?"Often granted for a two or three person panel early on. Stalls for a final loop where a committee has already blocked a shared hour, or where the panel exists deliberately for calibration.Ask at minute one who asks questions and in what order. Switch to speaker view so faces stop rearranging. Answer the person who asked, check the others once at the end.
3. A scheduled break"For sessions over about ninety minutes I do noticeably better with a short scheduled break. Could we build in five minutes at the midpoint?"The easiest yes here and almost never refused outright. What goes wrong is that it is forgotten on the day, which is what the 24-hour re-confirm is for.Take it anyway: "Could we take two minutes before the next section?" Nobody refuses. On a back-to-back loop the gap between sessions is your break, so do not spend it re-reading the job description.
4. Extra time on the take-home"Could I return the exercise by Friday rather than Wednesday, with the same scope?"Near frictionless when it is a calendar deadline. Much harder when the exercise is timed inside a platform, because the clock is part of the assessment. Platforms differ, so ask whether the extension must be set before the invite is generated.Cut scope, not quality. Ship two requirements completely and list the rest in the README as "not done, here is the approach I would take". That beats four half-finished features.
5. Written follow-up after"Could you confirm next steps and timings in writing after the call? I retain detail better from a written summary."Granted for next steps and dates. Stalls on feedback, because many employers have a standing policy against putting candidate feedback in writing. Split the ask and you get the easy half.Write your own summary within ten minutes of hanging up: the next step, the date named, the one thing they seemed unconvinced by. Then email "just checking I noted this correctly", which invites a written correction.
6. The agenda in advance"Could I have the format ahead of time: who I am meeting, their roles, how long each section runs, and whether any part is live coding?"The most reliably granted item, and standard practice at many companies. It stalls only with an unresponsive recruiter, and need not be framed as an adjustment at all.Mine the calendar invite, which usually lists attendees, and look each one up. Ask the same question verbally on the phone screen. Assume the worst case for the coding round.
7. Notes and looking away"I will be taking notes while we talk, so I may be looking down. It is not distraction."Not a request, a notification, and universally fine. Saying it once removes the eye-contact loop that otherwise runs underneath every answer.Use paper, not a second window. Alt-tabbing looks worse on camera and on a platform that logs focus changes it can be recorded as a tab switch. Paper never appears in a screen share.
8. One question at a time"If a question has several parts, could I take them one at a time? And I may ask you to repeat one, so I answer the question you actually asked."Granted verbally with no fuss, then quietly forgotten by question three when the interviewer is reading a script of compound questions. Expect to re-apply it yourself.Split them out loud: "There are two things in there, let me take the second one first." Interviewers read that as structure, and it buys the seconds you need to hold the first part.
9. Fixed start time and time of day"Could we fix an exact start time, and schedule mid-morning rather than late afternoon? An open-ended wait beforehand affects how well I perform."Granted for remote calls, where the slot is the slot. Onsite loops stall, because they run late by design. What you can nearly always get instead is somewhere other than open plan to wait in.Plan the wait rather than filling it. Rehearsing in reception burns the attention you need in the room. Bring one index card with three story titles, read only that, phone away.

The email that gets a yes

Subject: Interview adjustment request, [Role], [date]

Hi [Name],

Thanks for setting this up. I would like to request two small adjustments to the format. Is there an accommodations contact I should copy in, or are you the right person?

First, I take in questions more accurately when I can read them as well as hear them. Could each question be pasted into the chat as it is asked? Second, the loop runs three hours and I do noticeably better with a short scheduled break, so could we build in five minutes after the second session?

Neither changes the content of the interview or the time I spend answering.

Best regards,
[Your name]

Note what it leaves out: no diagnosis, no apology, no fifth ask smuggled into the last line. The sentence about not changing the content is doing real work, because the unspoken worry on the other side is fairness to other candidates, and settling that before it is raised removes the reason to escalate your email to someone slower.

The night before, when the answer was no

Refusals are often not refusals. They are silence, or a "we'll try". The preparation is the same either way, and it is close to the opposite of cramming.

  • One printed page, six lines, taped beside the monitor. Anything you have to scroll is something you will not open. Story titles only, never full stories: "Payments migration", "The disagreement with the PM", "The outage". A title retrieves the whole thing, and reading a written paragraph aloud is audible in your voice.
  • Rehearse three first sentences aloud. The freeze happens at sentence one, not sentence four. Once the opening is automatic the rest arrives on its own.
  • Fix your two live sentences in advance. The repeat-back, and "Could we take two minutes before the next section?". Neither needs permission from anyone, which is why they work as the fallback.
  • Set the room up tonight. Charger in, notifications off, water in reach, a pen that works, the meeting link already open. Each is a decision you do not want to make at three minutes past the hour.
  • Do not move your medication schedule around a calendar invite. Ask instead for a slot inside the window where you normally function best, which is ask nine. Past that it is a question for a clinician, not for a software company's website.

Where a copilot helps, and where it is the wrong tool

We sell a live assistant, so here is the honest boundary. An on-screen prompt helps with retrieval, the moment where the story exists but will not come out. It does nothing for the parts of an interview that need a process change, and for some people it makes things worse.

  • It is not a substitute for a granted adjustment. If you need the questions in writing, ask for the questions in writing. Reaching for software instead means going through the process without the change you were entitled to request, and the next round has the identical problem.
  • A second stream of text competes for attention. That is the specific risk here. If you read rather than glance, the overlay becomes a third participant. Hold P ghosts it, Ctrl+Alt+H hides it, and it works better as a prompt card than a script.
  • It cannot help where you have been asked not to use tools. Capture exclusion keeps the overlay out of Zoom, Teams, Meet, OBS and screenshots, which is a privacy property, not an anti-detection one. It does nothing against a webcam, a lockdown browser, a proctor in the room, eye tracking or a second-device camera. Where a process forbids assistance, the route is the accommodation request.
  • Windows 10 and 11 only. No macOS, no Linux, no mobile, no browser extension, so if your interview machine is a MacBook this is not an option at all.
