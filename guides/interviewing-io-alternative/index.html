interviewing.io alternative: what an hour of interview help actually costs

2026 · ~7 min read · OfferPilot AI

interviewing.io sells one hour of a working engineer's attention plus the debrief at the end. A live copilot subscription sells listening minutes on your own machine, metered only while the other person is talking. Normalized to cost per hour, those two sit roughly two orders of magnitude apart, and the table below runs that arithmetic against $39 Pro, $69 Ultimate and the $249 six-month pass. It also says the part a vendor would rather skip: there is nobody on the cheap side who can tell you your answer was mediocre.

What each side is actually selling

interviewing.io's product is a stranger. You book an anonymous mock with an engineer who interviews candidates as part of a real job, they run a round on you, and the value sits in the last stretch of the call where they say out loud what they would have written in a debrief. The price is a person's hourly rate with a marketplace on top, which is why it does not fall when you buy more of them. Paid mocks with working engineers are widely reported in the low hundreds of dollars per session, commonly cited in the region of $150 to $300 for a roughly hour-long slot depending on the interviewer's company and level. Treat that as a reported range checked 2026-08-31, not a quote. Their own booking page is the only authority, and per-interviewer rates have historically differed.

A live copilot sells something with no human in it at all. It listens to the interviewer's audio through system playback, transcribes on device, and drafts a speakable answer against your resume in roughly a second. Nobody watches you. Nothing is graded. It is priced like software, so the marginal hour costs nothing and the average hour keeps falling the more you use it. Those two cost curves are the entire comparison: one is flat forever, the other collapses toward zero.

Put $249 on the table and pick one. On the reported band above it buys a single mock with a working engineer, or two if you find someone at the low end and nothing goes wrong with scheduling. The same $249 buys the six-month pass, which allows up to six hours of listening a day for a hundred and eighty days. On volume that is not a close call, and it was never going to be. On what you need it is very close, because the first option contains a person who has run the loop and the second contains no person at all.

The hours are not the same size

One correction before the table means anything. Metering counts listening time only, so the clock advances while the copilot is receiving interviewer audio, not for the whole wall-clock session. A booked mock consumes an hour of your life and an hour of theirs. An hour of listening time is not an hour of interview.

Work it through on a 45-minute phone screen. If the interviewer speaks for about a third of it, briefing you, clarifying, pushing back on your approach, that is roughly 15 minutes on the meter, so one hour of listening covers around four calls of that shape. A system design round where they talk half the time burns closer to twenty or twenty-five minutes each. A quiet pairing round where they say a dozen words in total barely registers. Divide the per-hour numbers in the table by three or four for a rough per-interview cost: a well-used Pro month lands near fifty cents a round, a lightly used one under three dollars. OfferPilot AI is cheap per interview in a way a human hour structurally cannot be, and that is a fact about arithmetic, not about quality.

What $39 a month does not buy

Everything above is a budget argument, and budget arguments are the easy half. Here is the part that should decide it.

A copilot answers the question. It does not tell you whether you would have. That sounds academic until you have sat through a debrief where somebody says the thing nobody in your life was going to say: your first ten minutes were fine, then you stopped narrating and went silent for four minutes while you fought the bug, and that silence on its own was the no. Nothing in a subscription produces that sentence. There is no score, no recording of you, no trend line, no memory that last Tuesday went badly.

  • Calibration is a person, not a model. Someone running a loop at a company this quarter knows where the bar sits now, which candidates cleared it last month, and what the internal debrief form actually asks them to rate. A model knows what the internet wrote about that bar a while ago. The gap between those two is precisely what you are paying for.
  • The feedback is about you, not the answer. Any model produces a good answer. Only a human tells you that you restate every question for ninety seconds, start coding before agreeing on an approach, or say "obviously" right before each hard part.
  • A stranger will actually say it. Your friend at a big company will not tell you that you came across as arrogant. An anonymous interviewer with no relationship to protect will. The anonymity is the product, not a privacy setting.
  • Software never tells you no. It succeeds every time. Watching good answers appear on your screen for twelve months can leave you at the same skill level with a much stronger feeling of readiness, which is the worst possible combination on the day.

So the honest ordering. If you have never been through a real technical loop and cannot tell whether your own answers are any good, buy the human hour first. Spending a year of subscription money instead of one mock is a way of not finding out.

How to split a small budget

Name the failure mode before spending anything

Two different problems get treated as one. Either you do not know the material, or you do not know how you land. Test the first alone: can you solve an unseen medium problem in 25 minutes, out loud, with no hints? The second cannot be diagnosed alone, by definition. Spend money on whichever one you actually have.

Spend nothing while the answer is still "I do not know the material"

A paid mock that concludes "revise your fundamentals" is an expensive receipt for information you already had. Grind free problems, read editorials, and use the free 5 minutes a day, which needs no card, to check your reasoning against a drafted answer rather than to be rescued mid-call.

Book the human hour late, not early

Roughly two to three weeks before an onsite is the sweet spot. Early enough that you can still change something, late enough that you are fluent and the notes come back about judgment and communication instead of syntax. One well-timed mock beats three panicked ones in the same week.

Convert the debrief into a question bank the same day

Whatever they flagged is now your study list, and it expires fast because you will forget the exact wording. If they said your design answers skip capacity estimates, generate a bank for that company and role with the company Q&A generator and drill the named weakness instead of restarting a generic grind.

Buy the software for the month you interview, not the month you start studying

Pro and Ultimate bill monthly, so time the spend to the month with real calls in it. The six-month pass only pays off if you expect a search running past six months, which is a genuine possibility worth being honest with yourself about. Caps and prices sit on the plans page.

Where this comparison stops being useful

Do not run a copilot during a paid mock. You are buying a stranger's read on you, so feeding them an assisted answer means paying a couple of hundred dollars for calibrated feedback on a model's output. That is worthless to you. Hide the overlay with Ctrl+Alt+H or close it, and let the answer be as bad as it is. This is the one situation where the cheap tool actively destroys the value of the expensive one.

Windows 10 and 11 only. No macOS build, no Linux, no mobile app, no browser extension. interviewing.io runs in a browser on anything, so if you are on a Mac the comparison ends at the platform row and the rest of this page does not apply to you.

Capture exclusion is narrower than people assume. The overlay is excluded from screen capture at the operating system level, so it stays out of Zoom, Teams, Meet, OBS and screenshots while remaining visible on your own monitor. It does nothing about a webcam, a lockdown browser, a proctor watching you in the room, eye tracking, or a phone camera pointed at your desk. If a company has told you an assessment is unassisted, that is the rule, and no overlay setting changes it.

There is no feedback loop here at all. OfferPilot AI listens to the interviewer, never to you, so there is no filler count, no speaking pace, no delivery score and no judgment of how your answer landed. If your problem is that you do not know how you come across, this is the wrong purchase, and a mock interview, a coach, or a tool that records and reviews your own speech is the right one.

Buy the expensive hour for the truth, the cheap hours for the volume

Priced per hour the two barely belong in the same table, and per hour is not the unit that decides it. One purchase tells you something true about yourself that you cannot get anywhere else. The other makes every live round afterwards cost less than a coffee.
