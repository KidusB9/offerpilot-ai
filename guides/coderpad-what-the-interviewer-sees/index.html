Does CoderPad detect tab switching, and what does the interviewer see?

2026 · ~6 min read · OfferPilot AI

Yes, and the important part is the timing: in a live pad the interviewer is not reading a report afterwards, they are watching it happen. Focus leaving the pad raises a notice on their side while you are still away. Text pasted in from outside arrives highlighted in the shared editor, where both of you can see it land. In a CoderPad Screen assessment, dropping out of fullscreen raises its own alert after a short grace period. What none of it carries is a destination, so the signal is always that you left and what appeared next, never where you went.

Start with the baseline, because it is bigger than any alert. A live pad is a shared document, so the interviewer sees every keystroke as you make it: the variable you rename twice, the loop you write and delete, the twenty seconds the caret sits still. Running the code puts the same output pane in front of them. There is no submit button and no moment where your work becomes visible, because it already was. The alerts below cover only what happens outside that document.

The three alerts that fire while the session is still running

CoderPad splits into two products that behave differently, and mixing them up causes most of the confusion online. The collaborative pad is a live interview with a human at the other end of the document. Screen is the assessment you take alone. The first two signals apply to both, the third only to Screen.

Focus leaves the pad

The pad is a web page, so the trigger is the browser's own visibility and focus events. The tab becoming hidden, or the window losing keyboard focus, surfaces a notice on the interviewer's side, and the clock on it runs until you come back. It is a live nudge rather than a line in a file, which is why interviewers so often ask whether everything is alright four seconds after you Alt-Tab.

An outside paste, marked in the editor

Pasting is not treated as one thing. Copying a helper function from higher up in the pad and pasting it back is ordinary editing. Content arriving from outside the document is flagged in the editor itself, so the interviewer watching the same shared buffer sees a highlighted block appear the instant it drops in. Nothing to check later, no report to wait for.

Fullscreen exit on a Screen assessment

Where the hiring team has required fullscreen, leaving it does not flag instantly. A short grace period runs first, long enough that a stray Esc or an operating system dialog does not generate an incident on its own. Stay outside past it and the exit is alerted and recorded against the attempt, and repeats accumulate.

The exact wording, the length of that grace period and which of these a company has switched on depend on the plan and the settings the hiring team chose, so treat this as the shape of the behaviour rather than a fixed schema. The two sources underneath it do not change: standard browser events, plus the editor's own record of how text entered the document.

The quirk: opening the console reads as leaving

Developer tools take keyboard focus away from the document. Undocked they are a separate window, docked they are a panel your caret moves into, and either way the page stops being the focused thing. A candidate who hits F12 to read why a fetch is failing produces the same leave event as one who opened a second tab for the answer.

The people most likely to trip this are the ones you want to hire. Reflexively opening the console when a front-end pad misbehaves is a good instinct that looks identical to a bad one in the log.

Same for a chat toast that steals focus, a password manager popup, the machine auto-locking, and clicking a second monitor while the pad stays fully visible on the first. None of these arrive labelled as innocent.

The fix is verbal, not technical, and it works because a person is on the call. Say it before you do it: I am opening the console to check that error. An event the interviewer has already been told about stops being a signal, and looking something up is normal engineering behaviour. Silence is what makes a four minute gap interesting.

What playback replays afterwards, and what it never had

CoderPad keeps the pad's edit history and replays it as a scrubbable timeline, so someone who was not in the room can watch your solution appear at the speed it was written. That view is rich in ways the finished file is not. It shows the order you built things in, the long pause before you saw the trick, the function you wrote and deleted, and sixty working lines arriving in a single frame twenty seconds after focus came back.

What it never had is just as concrete. No screen recording, no view of your other tabs, no clipboard history saying where the text came from, no keystrokes outside the pad, no picture of you. A video call, if the round used one, is a separate stream on separate settings, not part of the replay. Playback records a document, not a person, which is why every flag CoderPad produces has the same shape: focus left, then this appeared. Never: you were here.

That is also why the raw count of leave events matters less than candidates fear. Fourteen two second losses across an hour is a laptop with notifications left on. One six minute loss starting right after the hard part loads, followed by a highlighted paste, is a story. In a live pad the interviewer usually just asks you to walk through the block, often within a minute of it landing.

Where OfferPilot AI is the wrong tool for this

Worth saying plainly on a page published by a company that sells an interview assistant. Our overlay is excluded from screen capture at the operating system level, so it does not appear in a Zoom, Teams or Meet share, in recordings or in screenshots. That property is irrelevant here. Focus detection does not look at pixels, it reads which window the operating system considers focused, so interacting with any overlay, or triggering the Code Copilot screenshot shortcut, moves focus off the pad and raises the identical notice. Capture exclusion also does nothing against webcam monitoring, a lockdown browser, eye tracking, a second-device camera or a human proctor.

A live pad is also the format where a pasted answer helps least. The interviewer reads the code as it appears and will ask you to change it, so anything you cannot explain and extend under questioning is worse than a half solution you wrote yourself.

A short pre-pad checklist

  • Keep the pad on one screen and put nothing you will click on the other, since clicking a second monitor blurs the pad while it stays visible.
  • Narrate anything that takes you out of the tab, the console included, before you do it.
  • Type your snippets, do not paste them. A template typed by hand is ordinary editing, the same text pasted from a notes app is highlighted.
  • If something interrupts you, say what it was at the time. An explained gap and an unexplained one read very differently.
