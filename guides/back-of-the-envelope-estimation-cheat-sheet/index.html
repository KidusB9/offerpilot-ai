Back-of-the-envelope estimation: the numbers to have memorised

2026 · ~7 min read · OfferPilot AI

Memorise six things and every estimation in a system design round becomes arithmetic you can do out loud. There are 100,000 seconds in a day (86,400, rounded up). One million events a day is about 10 per second. A metadata row is 100 bytes, a web-size photo 300 KB, a chat message 250 bytes. One KB per second is 30 GB a year. Memory is 100 ns, an SSD read 100 µs, a same-datacentre round trip 0.5 ms, a cross-continent one 150 ms. Peak is 3× average unless you have a reason to say otherwise. Below: the full table, three designs estimated longhand in under two minutes each, and how to round hard without sounding like you are guessing.

The whole table, one screen

You are not being tested on arithmetic. You are being tested on whether you can turn a product statement into a number that forces an architectural decision, in about ninety seconds, while talking. These are the inputs.

Two consequences are worth saying aloud when they come up. Memory is roughly a thousand times faster than an SSD, so a cache hit and a cache miss are different systems, not the same system slightly slower. And a cross-continent round trip at 150 ms already eats most of a 200 ms budget, so anything global has to be served near the user no matter how fast your code is.

Three designs, estimated longhand

Each of these is under two minutes spoken. Notice that each one produces a different kind of number, which is exactly why all three keep getting asked.

Photo-sharing feed: the bytes are the problem

Assume 500M daily users, each opening the feed 10 times, each page showing 10 photos. Reads: 5 billion feed loads a day, divided by 100,000, is 50k QPS average, 150k at peak. Uploads: one user in ten posts once a day, so 50M photos a day, which is 500 uploads a second. Storage: 50M times 2 MB is 100 TB a day, about 40 PB a year, and 120 PB once replicated three ways.

Now the number that decides the architecture. Egress is 50k feed loads a second, times 10 photos, times 300 KB, which comes to 150 GB/s, or 1.2 Tbps. At 10 Gbps a box, that is 120 servers doing nothing but pushing bytes, before any application logic exists. A CDN here is not an optimisation, it is the design. Meanwhile the metadata is 50M rows at 300 bytes, about 15 GB a day and 5 TB a year, which fits comfortably in one ordinary database. Say both numbers next to each other. Four orders of magnitude apart is the entire justification for splitting blobs from metadata, and it lands far better as a comparison than as two separate facts.

URL shortener: prove it is small, then move on

Assume 1M new links a day and a 100:1 read to write ratio, so 100M redirects. Writes are 10 a second. Reads are 100M over 100,000 seconds, so 1,000 QPS, 3,000 at peak. Storage: 1M times 200 bytes is 200 MB a day, 70 GB a year, so a decade of links is about 700 GB and fits on a single disk. Key space: base62 over 7 characters is 627, roughly 3.5 trillion codes. Even 6 characters gives 57 billion, which at a million a day is over 150 years of supply.

Bandwidth is a redirect response of a few hundred bytes, so 1,000 QPS is well under a megabyte a second. The honest conclusion, and interviewers like hearing it stated, is that one well-provisioned machine could serve this workload, and 30 days of links at 6 GB fits entirely in RAM. So you say it: capacity is not the interesting constraint here, and I would rather spend the remaining time on p99 latency for a globally distributed redirect, on generating keys without coordination or collisions, and on abuse and expiry. That reframing is what the question was fishing for all along.

Chat app: connections, not bytes

Assume 500M daily users sending 40 messages each: 20 billion messages a day, which over 100,000 seconds is 200k messages a second, 600k at peak. With a mix of one-to-one threads and small groups, call average fanout 2, so 400k deliveries a second. Bytes are almost nothing: 200k times 250 B is 50 MB/s of ingest, four orders of magnitude below the photo app at a similar user count.

Two numbers actually shape this design. First, concurrency. If 20% of users are online at peak that is 100 million open connections, and at a defensible 250k sockets a box that is 400 connection servers, plus a registry mapping each user to the box holding their socket. Second, retention. Keeping every message at 250 bytes is 5 TB a day and about 2 PB a year before replication, whereas deleting after delivery leaves only the undelivered backlog, perhaps a few hundred GB. One product decision moves storage by three orders of magnitude, and naming that trade rather than silently assuming it is what scores.

Rounding hard without sounding sloppy

Aggressive rounding is a seniority signal when it is narrated and a red flag when it is silent. The difference sits almost entirely in the sentence wrapped around the number.

Sounds like guessingSounds deliberate
"Let us say about a hundred thousand seconds in a day.""86,400 seconds, I will use 100,000. That makes every QPS number I say roughly 15% low, and I will flag it if we get near a threshold."
"Photos are, I do not know, a couple of megabytes?""I will budget 2 MB a photo including the thumbnail and web-size derivatives. If it is really 5 MB the storage triples, but the CDN decision does not change."
"That comes to roughly 47,300 queries per second.""Call it 50k. I need to know whether this is ten boxes or a thousand, and a factor of two does not change that answer."
"Assume 500 million daily users.""I will assume 500M daily users and 10 opens each. If it is 50 opens my read path goes to 250k QPS, and I would want the CDN either way, so that input is not load bearing."
Silence, and then a number appears."20 billion a day, divided by 100,000 seconds, is 200,000 a second." Every intermediate said aloud, with the units attached to it.

Three habits carry the whole thing. Declare the precision you need before you calculate, because "one box or a thousand" licenses everything that follows. Round the numerator and the denominator in opposite directions where you can, and say that you did, so the errors partly cancel. And sanity check the output against something you already know: 500 photo uploads a second for a 500M-user product feels about right, and if you had produced 500,000 you would want to find the missing factor of a thousand before the interviewer does. Say "let me sanity check that" out loud. Nobody has ever lost points for it.

One more rule saves whole minutes. If a number does not change a decision, compute it, say it is small, and stop. "Metadata is a few TB a year, which is nothing, so I will not dwell on it" is a better use of ten seconds than a careful derivation of a figure that alters nothing downstream.

Where a live copilot is the wrong tool for this bit

This part of the interview is close to the worst case for live assistance, and it is worth being straight about that. Estimation is you talking for ninety seconds while the interviewer is silent, so a tool that listens to interviewer audio has almost nothing to work with. Interview Copilot transcribes the interviewer on-device and drafts a speakable answer in about a second, which fits behavioural rounds and rapid-fire technical questions well. It does not fit an arithmetic monologue you are meant to be narrating at a whiteboard, and glancing away mid-calculation is more visible than a thinking pause. The rest of the limits, plainly:

  • Windows 10 and 11 only. No macOS, no Linux, no mobile, no browser extension.
  • Capture exclusion covers screen sharing and screen recording, and nothing else. The overlay is excluded from screen capture at the operating system level, so it does not appear in Zoom, Teams, Meet, OBS or screenshots while staying visible on your own monitor. It does nothing about a webcam, a lockdown browser, eye tracking, a second-device camera, or a person sitting in the room.
  • Numbers you did not derive collapse on the follow-up. "Why 3× for peak?" and "what happens if photos are 5 MB?" arrive immediately, and a recited figure has no answer to either.
  • The rules belong to the company. Some interviews permit documentation and AI assistance and some do not. Read the instructions, and ask when they are ambiguous.

The useful preparation here is not live at all. It is drilling the table above until the conversions are reflexive, then running each of the three designs against a two-minute timer until you stop reaching for a calculator.

How to drill it

Pick four prompts, say a photo feed, a shortener, a chat app and a rate limiter, and estimate each one aloud in two minutes with a timer in view. Then run the same four inside the 45-minute clock, because estimation that overruns steals time from the deep dive where the marks actually sit.
