How to hide notifications while screen sharing on Windows 11

2026 · ~6 min read · OfferPilot AI

Press Windows key + I, click System, click Notifications, and switch Do not disturb on. It is the first control under the master Notifications toggle. The one-click version, and the one worth memorising, is Windows key + N followed by the bell icon in the top right of the notification panel. Then the part that catches people out: the automatic rule named When I'm duplicating my display does not fire when you share a screen in Zoom or Teams. Sharing is not duplicating, and Windows cannot tell the difference because it never sees the share at all.

The exact click path, in order

Windows key + I, System, Notifications

System sits near the top of the left rail, Notifications a few entries down the right pane. The page opens with a master Notifications toggle at the top and Do not disturb immediately beneath it. Those are two different switches. The master toggle kills everything, including behaviour you will want back later, so leave it alone and use the one below it.

Switch Do not disturb on

Banners stop instantly, with no restart and no sign out. Confirm it took: the bell in the taskbar corner picks up a small crossed-out mark, and the header of the notification panel shows the same struck-through bell. Messages keep arriving. They queue silently in the notification center instead of drawing themselves across your shared screen.

Open Set priority notifications and cut the exceptions

This link sits directly under the Do not disturb switch, and it is the step almost everyone skips. Priority items are allowed through Do Not Disturb by design. Out of the box that includes Show incoming calls, including VoIP and Show reminders, regardless of app used, plus any apps already on the list underneath. An incoming call banner names the caller. Turn both off for a call you are sharing.

Expand Turn on do not disturb automatically

Five checkboxes live here. Tick During these times, set a start and end that genuinely covers your interview block, and set the repeat to weekdays. This is the only automatic rule that dependably applies to a screen share, because it keys off the clock rather than off something Windows has to detect.

Clear the notification center before you share, not after

Windows key + N, then Clear all. Everything Do Not Disturb suppressed is still sitting in that panel. Open the calendar flyout mid-call to check a date and the whole backlog appears at once, sender names and message previews included, which is a worse leak than the single banner you were trying to avoid.

Why "When I'm duplicating my display" never fires during a share

The wording is a trap. Duplicating your display means the Windows display mode you set with Windows key + P, the one used for a projector or a meeting room television, where a single desktop is painted onto two panels. It has nothing to do with sending pixels over a network. Zoom, Teams, Meet and Webex capture the desktop through the graphics stack, and Windows treats that like any other application reading the screen. No display mode changes, so no rule is triggered.

Plug in a projector and Do Not Disturb switches itself on. Share your screen with twelve people and nothing happens. That asymmetry is the whole reason this page exists.

Label wording drifts between Windows 11 builds, so you may see When duplicating my display without the pronoun, and the full screen entry sometimes carries a parenthetical about duplication. The behaviour is the same. If your Notifications page reads differently from the table above, trust your page and match by meaning rather than by string.

The leaks Do Not Disturb does not close

Do Not Disturb governs exactly one channel: notifications handed to the Windows notification system. A surprising amount of what appears on your screen never goes through it.

  • Apps that draw their own banners. Microsoft Teams has a Notification style setting under Settings, Notifications and activity, offering a Windows style and a Teams built-in style. On the built-in setting Teams paints the banner itself, so Windows Do Not Disturb never sees it and cannot suppress it. Set the style to Windows, or set your Teams presence to Do not disturb, or do both.
  • Slack, twice over. Pause notifications inside Slack as well: click your avatar, then Pause notifications, or type /dnd 60 in any channel. Even paused, the window keeps changing. Channel names go bold, the mention badge climbs, huddle invitations land in the sidebar, and a shared Slack window shows every bit of it.
  • Window titles and browser tabs. A tab reading (7) Slack or (3) Inbox is drawn inside the page, in the window you are sharing. No Windows setting touches it. Close those tabs, or share from a second browser profile that is signed into nothing.
  • Taskbar badges and previews. The unread count on the Mail or Teams icon keeps climbing. While you are in Settings, Personalisation, Taskbar deserves a visit too: the Widgets button rewrites its own headline text on the taskbar with no notification involved at all.
  • Sounds. Do Not Disturb silences the suppressed Windows notifications, but an app drawing its own alert plays its own sound. If you ticked share system audio, the room hears the ping even though nobody sees the banner.
  • Windows system prompts. Restart required, low battery, low disk space, a USB device connected. Behaviour under Do Not Disturb varies and several of these are engineered to be hard to ignore. Plug the laptop in and defer updates instead of trusting a setting to hold them back.

Test rather than assume. Start a meeting with yourself, share the screen you plan to share, join from your phone, then send yourself a Slack DM and a Teams message from another account. Ninety seconds, and you learn which of your apps route through Windows and which paint their own pixels. That answer differs by app version, which is exactly why no generic list can settle it for you.

Where this stops being the right tool

Do Not Disturb suppresses interruptions. It is not a privacy layer, and it does nothing about whatever is already on screen when you press share: an open document, a bookmarks bar, an address bar that autocompletes somewhere personal, a desktop covered in filenames. If the worry is what is visible rather than what pops up, the fix is a tidy desktop and a single window share instead of a full screen share, not a notifications setting.

The short version

  • Windows key + N, then the bell. Two inputs, works from inside a meeting window, and no default hotkey binds Do Not Disturb directly.
  • Schedule it rather than remember it. During these times is the only automatic rule that covers a share. The duplication and full screen rules do not.
  • Check the priority list. Incoming calls and reminders come through Do Not Disturb on purpose, and a call banner carries a name.
  • Silence the apps too, then clear the panel. Teams notification style set to Windows, Slack paused, noisy tabs closed, notification center emptied before you click share.

Set the schedule once and everything above collapses into a ten second check before you present.
