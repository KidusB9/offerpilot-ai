Using a second monitor during a video interview: what the camera sees

2026 · ~6 min read · OfferPilot AI

The second monitor is not the problem. Zoom, Teams, Meet and Webex each capture one display at a time, so the panel with your notes on it never reaches the stream. Your face is the half nobody configures. A screen placed beside the webcam pulls your eyes thirty to forty degrees off axis in a repeating left-to-right rhythm, and it is the rhythm, not the glance, that reads as reading. Put the same material directly under the lens and it becomes a ten to fifteen degree downward flick, which looks like the thing everybody does while thinking.

One line of arithmetic decides the whole setup

How far off axis a glance lands is not a matter of taste. It is arctan(offset / viewing distance), where the offset is the distance from the lens to the thing you are looking at. Measure two numbers with a tape and you know what the interviewer's tile will show. At a typical 60 cm desk distance:

Where the reference material sitsOffset from the lensAngle at 60 cmWhat a video tile shows
Top third of a screen mounted under the webcam8 cm belowabout 8°Eyelids dip. The iris barely moves and the head does not move at all.
Middle of that same screen15 cm belowabout 14°A short downward flick, head still. Hard to tell from looking at your own hands.
Laptop panel below an external webcam clipped to a monitor above it25 cm belowabout 23°A clear look down, with a small chin drop on anything longer than a second or two.
Near edge of a monitor placed to one side35 cm acrossabout 30°Eyes travel sideways and stay there. The head starts to follow.
Centre of that side monitor50 cm acrossabout 40°A head turn. Not a glance any more.
Phone on a stand next to the keyboard30 cm down, 20 cm acrossabout 31°Down and sideways together, plus the separate tell of a lit object being looked at.

A monitor at your elbow is the worst option available and one under your chin is close to the best, and the difference is where the hardware sits, not what is on it. Sliding a document up under the bezel rather than leaving it centred buys another six or seven degrees for nothing.

Horizontal reads as reading, vertical reads as thinking

Three things make a sideways glance louder than a downward one, and they stack.

Past roughly twenty to thirty degrees, the head joins in. The threshold varies by person, so test your own: hold your head still, look at something forty degrees to the side, and notice how fast the neck wants to help. It matters because on a small tile your iris is a handful of pixels and survives compression badly, while a head turn moves the whole frame.

Reading has a metronome. Eyes do not slide along a line, they jump along it in short hops and then sweep back to the left margin, over and over, on a fairly regular beat. That repeating sweep is the signature, and it is why people say someone "looks like they are reading" without being able to point at a single moment. Thinking produces one movement out and one back, nothing rhythmic in between.

The eyelid hides a downward glance and exposes a sideways one. Look down and the upper lid follows the eye, so less of it shows. Look hard to one side and a crescent of white sclera appears beside the iris, the highest-contrast edge on your face, legible even at thumbnail size.

You can move the monitor. You can also shorten the sweep. Both are free.

The sweep is worth attacking directly, because the same arithmetic governs it. Reading edge to edge across a 27-inch panel at 60 cm is about 26 degrees of travel each way. Put the same document in a 15 cm column and that drops to about 7, small enough that a viewer sees a fixed gaze rather than a scanning one.

The five-minute placement, in order

Put the camera above the screen you read from

Not the other way round. If the webcam clips to the top monitor and your reference sits on the laptop panel below it, the offset is the whole height of the upper monitor. Move the webcam down onto the reading screen, or use the laptop's own camera and stack nothing above it.

Drag the meeting window under the lens too

The step people skip. On a 27-inch panel, an interviewer's face parked in the bottom right corner is already about 30 cm off axis, so you read as looking away with no second monitor in the room. Use speaker view, shrink the window, move it to the top centre of the camera screen.

Raise the lens to eye height

A low camera means you are already looking down at the interviewer, so a glance below it compounds rather than cancels. Books under a laptop work as well as a monitor arm. Sitting normally, your eyeline should meet the lens without your chin moving.

Park the text in the top third, in a narrow column

Top-align the window instead of maximising it, set a line length of roughly 15 cm, and raise the font size until you can read it without leaning in. Leaning forward is its own tell, and no placement fixes it.

Record thirty seconds and watch it small

Use the Windows Camera app, or start a meeting with nobody in it and record locally. Read from your reference as you would in the round, then play it back shrunk to tile size. Sideways travel and small head turns are obvious on replay in a way they never are live.

If the screen holds a question bank rather than a document, the geometry is identical but the wording matters more, because six-word prompts you take in with one fixation beat sentences you have to read. What none of this fixes

Worth being blunt, because the two halves of this question get confused constantly. Capture exclusion is a display-side feature: it tells the operating system that a window may not be recorded, so the window is absent from a Zoom, Teams or Meet share, from OBS and from screenshots, while staying visible on your own monitor. The OfferPilot AI overlay works that way, on every plan including the free one. It does nothing whatsoever about a webcam. It does nothing about a person in the room with you, a phone camera pointed at your desk, or a proctoring tool watching your face. Nothing that hides a window from a screen share can also hide your eyes.

That sets the boundary for the rest of it. On a proctored assessment a second monitor is frequently disallowed outright, and many proctoring tools read how many displays are connected, then flag the setup or require you to unplug the spare before you begin. Those rules are normally stated up front, so read them, and where they are ambiguous, ask. On a monitored exam ours is the wrong tool and we would rather say so. It is Windows 10 and 11 only in any case.

The honest limit on the placement advice is that geometry buys a quieter glance, not invisibility. If you need forty words on screen, you will be seen reading forty words, and the second tell comes with it: the pause. A glance, two seconds of silence, then a fluent paragraph is a very different signal from a glance during speech. That is a preparation problem, not a hardware one. Treat software gaze correction, such as the eye contact effect in NVIDIA Broadcast, with equal caution: it can snap visibly when your head moves.

The short version

  • Under the lens, never beside it. Fifteen centimetres below the camera is about 14 degrees. Fifty to the side is about 40, and a visible head turn.
  • Narrow the column, not just the glance. Full-width text on a 27-inch panel is a 26 degree sweep each way. A 15 cm column is about 7.
  • Move the interviewer's window too. A face in the corner of a big monitor makes you look away all round, second screen or not.
  • Screen-share privacy and webcam privacy are unrelated problems. Solving one tells you nothing about the other.
