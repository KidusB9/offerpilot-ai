Panic attack during an interview: what to say and do in the moment

2026 · ~6 min read · OfferPilot AI

Say one short sentence out loud instead of going silent, because the silence is what makes it accelerate. "Give me a second, I want to answer that properly" buys ten to fifteen seconds. If you need longer, ask plainly: "Could we take two minutes? I'll be right back," then mute, breathe out for longer than you breathe in, and come back. The pause is far less memorable to the interviewer than it feels to you.

This is not medical advice. It is a set of scripts for the ten minutes you are currently in, written for people who already know what a panic attack feels like on them. Recurring attacks are a conversation for a doctor.

The first sixty seconds

Out-breath longer than in-breath

Four counts in through the nose, six to eight out through the mouth. Resist the one big deep breath: hyperventilation is over-breathing, so more air worsens the dizziness.

Say something within five seconds

The content barely matters. A spoken sentence ends the dead air and gives your body a task. Silence does the opposite: you start hearing your own heartbeat.

Anchor the body somewhere concrete

Feet flat, palms pressed down on your thighs, jaw unclenched, one sip of cold water. Faster than reasoning yourself calm while adrenaline is still arriving.

Then choose: continue, pause or stop

In that order. Most attacks are handled at "continue" with one buying sentence. Escalate only as far as you need, and treat stopping as legitimate.

Symptom to response, with the literal words

Column three is what you say out loud, at normal volume, before you stop talking. Column four is what to do while it buys you room.

The exact wording for asking for a break

The commonest mistake is hedging so hard that you invite a refusal. "Would it maybe be possible to perhaps take a short break, if that's okay?" sounds declinable. State it in one sentence and act without waiting for an answer.

  • Video call: "Could we take two minutes? I'll be right back." Then mute, camera off, go.
  • Phone screen: "Can I put you on hold for a moment? Thank you."
  • In person: "Could you point me to the bathroom? I'll be back in two minutes."
  • To name it without disclosing anything: "I'm feeling a bit unwell, I need a short break."

Hold yourself to the two minutes, because a break that stretches to eight is harder to come back from. Close it with one sentence and do not reopen it: "Thanks for waiting. You'd asked about the migration project, shall I pick up there?" One apology is professional, three keep everyone inside the event you are leaving.

For formal adjustments, a written question list, extra time or a scheduled break, ask in advance and in writing. Disclosure rules vary by country and employer, so check what applies where you are.

Muting for the seconds you need not to be heard

Breathing down is easier when you are certain nobody can hear it. Two separate controls are involved, and people mix them up under pressure.

First, the microphone in the meeting app. On Windows the defaults are usually Alt+A in Zoom, Ctrl+Shift+M in Teams and Ctrl+D in Google Meet, with camera toggles on Alt+V, Ctrl+Shift+O and Ctrl+E. Two caveats matter more than the shortcuts: they can be reassigned, and in most clients they fire only when the meeting window has focus, which catches people out. Check yours the day before.

Second, a live assistant has its own listening control. In OfferPilot AI, holding M pauses the mic the copilot is using for as long as you hold the key. Two honest notes. It does not mute you inside Zoom, Teams or Meet, which are separate controls, so use both if the interviewer is to hear nothing. And because the copilot meters listening time, not wall-clock time, paused seconds do not spend your daily allowance. Hold P makes the overlay see-through and Ctrl+Alt+H hides it, if looking at it is part of the problem.

The email for when you have to stop

Sometimes ending the call is right. Send this within a few hours, while the interviewer still has the conversation in mind.

Subject: Following up on today's interview, [Role]

Hi [Name],

Thank you for your time this morning, and apologies for having to end our conversation early. I became unwell and could not continue.

I am still very interested in the [Role] position and would welcome the chance to finish the interview. I am free Thursday before midday, Friday afternoon, or any time Monday, and happy to work around your schedule. If it is simpler, I would gladly answer the remaining questions in writing.

Thanks for your understanding.

Best regards,
[Your name]

Note what it does. "Unwell" is a complete explanation, and you need not expand on it. Concrete windows halve the effort on their side, where "let me know what suits" adds work. The written route is often the one a busy manager takes.

Where a copilot helps, and where it is the wrong tool

Worth being straight, since we sell one. Much of interview panic is fear of the blank moment, and your own material on screen removes some of that. It does not touch the underlying condition, and sometimes it makes things worse:

  • It is not treatment. Software is not a clinician, and no overlay substitutes for a GP or a therapist.
  • Another screen is another demand on attention. If tunnel vision or detachment is your main symptom, a second stream of text competes with the interviewer. Ghost it with hold P and glance rather than read.
  • If the anxiety is about the software, do not run the software. Some people are calmer with nothing running.
  • Screen-share exclusion has limits. The overlay is excluded from screen capture at the operating-system level, so it is absent from Zoom, Teams, Meet, OBS and screenshots. It does not defeat a webcam, a lockdown browser, an in-person proctor, eye tracking or a second-device camera, and nothing here is about getting around a monitored assessment.
  • Windows 10 and 11 only. No macOS, no Linux, no mobile, no browser extension.

The genuinely useful preparation is duller than any of that. Rehearse three or four answers aloud until the opening sentence is automatic, because that is where people freeze, and a company-specific question bank gives you the right ones to drill. Then write your break sentence on a sticky note on your monitor, so the words exist outside your head when your head stops cooperating.

If the panic arrives with the coding round, seeing the problem broken down removes one source of it, which is what the Interview Copilot and Code Copilot are for. Neither replaces the sentence you say out loud.
